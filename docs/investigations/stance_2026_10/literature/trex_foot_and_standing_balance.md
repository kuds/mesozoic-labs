# Longer toes cannot cure a forward lean

The published record points one way. A standing T. rex, like the living birds descended from its lineage, carried its weight on the thick proximal parts of its digits, close to the toe joints, with its centre of mass about 30–45% of a femur length ahead of the hip. Human and robot balance research also agrees that robustness comes from the distance between where the pressure sits and the edge the foot would tip over, not from foot length. Read against that evidence, the project's mid-training drift onto the toes is a control problem, not an anatomy problem. No reward term pays for fore-aft placement. The policy's two foot channels report each foot's total load (pad and digit sensors summed) but not where on the foot it sits. A left-right mirror loss cannot act on a fore-aft lean, because a symmetric lean is its own mirror image. And extending the load-bearing support forward (r10) only moved the edge the policy drifted toward. Two steps need no approval and come first: log the stability margin and the pad/digit load split through training, and choose checkpoints by margin and action-noise survival rather than reward, with about eight seeds per arm so that a difference in clean rate means something. Behind them sit options that need approval and have direct precedent in the literature: a fore-aft load signal for the critic or the actor; a floor on exploration noise, or training with injected action noise; and, if walking points back to stance under the 2026-10-16 rule, a CoP- or ZMP-centring term like those used on iCub and Unitree H1-2. Another look at the foot and ankle is worthwhile as a bounded measurement audit, not a third redesign. On the main-branch model the centre of mass already sits 42% of femur length ahead of the hip, and the home pose puts it within 0.10 of the pad centre, so mass distribution is not forcing the lean. The audit's main value is putting every figure in one frame. In that frame the pad centre sits at about 0.31 of the model's footprint length from the rear, the 0.80 gate bar at about 0.55 and the drifted stance at about 0.61. That stance is forward of both the human quiet-stance analogue (about 0.46) and the bird analogue (load under the toe joint, about 0.04). Three real unknowns remain: no published ankle or toe range of motion for T. rex, no measured standing pressure data for any large theropod or ratite, and no RL ablation of a CoP term in standing.

## T. rex loaded its proximal digits, with its centre of mass ahead of the hip

The skeletal and track records agree on the basic plan of the foot. The dinosaur foot was **primitively digitigrade, with the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joints raised above the ground**. Long tracks once read as flat-footed walking are now reinterpreted as a digitigrade foot sinking into soft mud ([Lallensack, Farlow & Falkingham 2022](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf)). The T. rex metatarsus is an arctometatarsus: a long, narrow unit in which the central MT III is pinched between its neighbours. Ligaments bind the three bones distally, and their attachment scars, placed to resist torsion and splay, are present in adult tyrannosaurids but absent in allosauroids of similar size ([Surring et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.18435/vamp29387)). A "tensile keystone" reading has those ligaments drawing the bones together early in stance ([Snively & Russell 2003](https://doi.org/10.1002/jmor.10059)). In adults, **MT III is 650–680 mm long against femora of 1100–1280 mm**, a ratio of 0.52–0.60, and the tibia is 0.90–0.93 of femur length. The juvenile LACM 23845 has a tibia as long as its femur and an MT III at 0.62 of it ([Persons & Currie 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep19828); the ratios are arithmetic on their Table 1). Three digits carried the weight, with two, three and four pads on digits II, III and IV ([Lockley, Gierliński & Lucas 2011](https://juraparkbaltow.pl/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Lockley-Gierlinski-and-Lucas-2011.pdf)). The rear of a theropod track is normally the **pad under the digit IV MTP joint**; the MTP pads of digits II and III were usually held clear of the ground ([Lallensack et al. 2022](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf), citing Farlow et al. 2000). The hallux sat high and medial and left marks only in occasional prints ([McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613); [Boeye et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.252139)).

Track depth shows where the weight went. In well-preserved tyrannosaur trackways **the deepest areas lie around the proximal pedal phalanges**, one centred on digits II and III and a second on digit IV ([Boeye et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.252139)). Claw impressions taper strongly ([McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)), and in the ostrich the claw works as a traction anchor at push-off rather than as a support ([Schaller et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)). If r10's digit contacts carry weight out to the claw tips, that runs against both lines of evidence. Large tyrannosaurid tracks are 50–86 cm long. Bellatoripes is 62 cm long, with a total digit spread generally no greater than 80° (the III–IV angle wider than II–III), in a narrow trackway left by an animal with an estimated hip height of 2.3–2.87 m ([McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)).

Every whole-body reconstruction puts the centre of mass (COM) ahead of and below the hip. A five-specimen study places the most plausible adult COM **30–45% of femur length in front of the hip (44–61% for Sue) and 22–50% of femur length below it**. Its minimal models put the COM about 0.55 m ahead of the hip (0.80 m for Sue) ([Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)). Bates and colleagues obtain 0.468 m for MOR 555 and 0.572 m for BHI 3033. Even their models with enlarged tails and shrunken chests keep the COM "comfortably in front of the hip joint" ([Bates et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004532)). A tail with a 40% broader cross-section shifts the modelled COMs 5–15% back toward the hip socket; only in two other large theropods, Acrocanthosaurus and Sinraptor, did the COM reach the socket ([Snively et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6432)). A 2023 analysis warns against a simple "theropod COM near the hip, bird COM forward" split, and finds tail mass strongly correlated with fore-aft COM (Rho = −0.989) ([Macaulay et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37317-y)). The minimal models' segment shares are head 4.1–11.1%, neck 3.2–7.3%, tail 10–21%, thigh 12.6–17.4%, shank 4.3–5.9% and foot 1.3–2.9% of body mass ([Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)).

Posture studies converge on a narrow target. Large theropods "used fairly upright limb orientations". Ankle extensor support mattered in every taxon studied, and hip extensor capacity "appears to be a crucial limit" ([Hutchinson 2004](https://doi.org/10.1002/jmor.10240)). Slightly flexed poses generate the moments needed to stand better than fully columnar ones ([Hutchinson et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1666/0094-8373%282005%29031%5B0676%3AAOHMMA%5D2.0.CO%3B2)). Joint and force constraints leave T. rex "a narrow band of poses that are neither very upright nor strongly flexed" ([Gatesy, Bäker & Hutchinson 2009](https://doi.org/10.1671/039.029.0213)). Cancellous bone in Daspletosaurus points to a subvertical femur at mid-stance, and that model was posed with the knee near the line of the vertical ground force ([Bishop et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777)). Because the COM sits ahead of the hip, T. rex "required some flexion in the limbs to stand still" ([Hutchinson, Ng-Thow-Hing & Anderson 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2007.01.023)). For the project, the consequence is direct. A standing T. rex placed its feet under its COM and paid for it with hip-extensor torque. No study supports a forward lean onto the toes, and such a lean moves the ground force further ahead of the hip and knee, which raises extensor demand rather than lowering it.

The joint-level evidence is thin, which limits what a foot audit can settle. **No published numeric ankle or MTP range of motion exists for T. rex or for any large non-avian theropod.** The GaitSym T. rex used sagittal hinges whose ranges were "estimated directly from the skeleton" but not published, and it modelled the feet as contact spheres on the digits ([Sellers et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3420)). A Daspletosaurus model needed an MTP reserve actuator of at least 27 × body weight × leg length, because the reconstructed digital flexors could not control the toe joint on their own ([Bishop et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777)). Simulations of Coelophysis attribute their restricted MTP angles partly to using a single contact sphere per foot ([Bishop et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abi7348)). The only passive-tissue evidence specific to tyrannosaurs is the arctometatarsus ligaments, which support modelling the metatarsus as one stiff segment rather than adding a degree of freedom. The tail was plausibly held up by pre-strained interspinous ligaments, giving a natural frequency near 0.66 Hz ([van Bijlert, van Soest & Schulp 2021](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.201441)). In Coelophysis, making the tail passive had a negligible effect, and tail length mattered more than tail mass ([Bishop et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abi7348)). The definitive pes measurements for Sue are in Brochu's 2003 monograph ([Brochu 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724634.2003.10010947)), which the research did not reach. A foot audit should begin by obtaining that table.

## Birds keep the load under the toe joint and brace only one way

Birds are the closest living test of where a theropod puts its weight, and in every measured case the load sits near the toe joint, not at the tips. In flamingos standing quietly on one leg, the **centre of pressure (CoP) sits directly under the tarsometatarsophalangeal joint (the bird equivalent of the MTP)**, which minimises joint torque. Sway shrinks to a 95% CoP radius of 3.2 mm, about 5–10% of foot length ([Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)). Running ostriches carry **77% (solid ground) to 84% (sand) of vertical force on digit III** and 14–23% on digit IV, with the claw taking 1.5–2.4% at toe-off. The CoP starts at the lateral or middle part of digit III and reaches the claw only after about 70% of stance ([Zhang et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/)). Digit IV acts as a balance outrigger, and modulating its pressure corrects balance ([Schaller et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)). Ligaments keep the ostrich MTP joint permanently off the ground, even after all muscles are removed ([Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)). Emus rising from sitting keep the CoP inside a base formed by the tarsometatarsus and the digits ([Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519)). A policy that settles with its weight toward the front of the digits is therefore copying the bird push-off phase, not bird quiet stance. The caveat is large: **no pressure data exist for quiet two-legged standing in any ratite**, and the flamingo result comes from a small wading bird on one leg.

Birds do have passive standing aids, but each one braces in only one direction and only in a particular posture. Two flamingo cadavers held their body weight on one leg with no muscle activity, yet "rapidly became unstable" when posed on two legs. The stay engages only when three conditions hold: the COM is ahead of the knee, joint limits oppose the resulting moments, and the foot sits medial to the hip ([Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)). The ostrich ankle (intertarsal) joint has an engage-disengage mechanism. It snaps through near 115° and is fully engaged near 168°, and during ground contact the joint never flexes below about 164° in walking or about 160° in running ([Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)). Its passive moment, about 16 N·m in a loaded limb, is roughly an order of magnitude below the ankle moments emus need to stand up, which are 0.16–0.22 in units of body weight × leg length ([Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519); the scaling is rough arithmetic in the research notes). It works as a detent that holds an engaged posture, not as weight support. Whether non-avian theropods had such a mechanism is unproven. For a model, the lesson is that bird-style passive help disappears exactly when posture drifts out of its engaged range, which is what a forward lean onto the toes would do.

When a bird's COM shifts, it re-poses the limb instead of leaning. Chickens stand with the COM "vertically aligned with the feet" ([LeBlanc et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153477)). Chickens fitted with artificial tails stood with the femur about 40% more vertical and walked in a more hip-driven way, which the authors liken to non-avian theropods ([Grossi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0088458)). Guinea fowl recover from perturbations through limb posture and the intrinsic mechanics of their distal muscles. After an unexpected drop, the limb's contact angle explained most of the variation in limb loading ([Daley & Biewener 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1622881)). A proximal-to-distal gradient has been proposed, in which load-sensitive ankle and toe muscles do the fast correcting ([Daley, Felix & Biewener 2007](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/running-stability-enhanced-proximo-distal-gradient-joint-neuromechanical)), but a later treadmill belt-speed study did not support it as a general rule ([NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10334289)).

Bird balance sensing points to the pelvis and the distal muscles, not the sole. Bird leg skin has vibration-sensitive Herbst corpuscles, interpreted as a "warning device" for ground vibration ([Dorward & McIntyre 1971](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1971.sp009650)), but **no study shows birds using plantar pressure or CoP for balance**. The balance-specific evidence points instead to two sources. One is the lumbosacral organ between the hips. In a physical guinea-fowl model, sensors placed there picked up foot disturbances faster and better than head sensors ([Urbina-Meléndez et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00038)), and a quail study models the organ as a mass-spring-damper ([Kamska, Daley & Badri-Spröwitz 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa037)). Even so, "all hypotheses of LSO function lack conclusive evidence" ([Stanchak et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa024)). The other source is load-sensitive feedback from the distal muscles: guinea fowl whose calf-muscle stretch reflex had been removed kept limb force elevated for three strides after an obstacle, where intact birds recovered within one ([Schwaner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.245199)). The project's torso orientation and pelvis IMU signals roughly fill the lumbosacral organ's role. The avian input the model lacks is a direct reading of distal load.

The nearest published RL analogue is OstrichRL, a MuJoCo ostrich with 120 muscles. It observes joint states, muscle states and body heights but no contact forces. Under a simple forward-velocity reward it repeatedly learned to run with its **toes "unrealistically bent backwards" and its ankles straight**, and the standing segments of its motion-tracking clips were among the slowest to learn. The authors fixed this with model changes and motion-capture tracking rewards, not with new observations ([La Barbera et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.06061)). In the OpenSim ostrich model, the distal joints needed reserve torques, and hip abduction/adduction reserves supplied more than half of that joint's moment, which the authors read as standing in for unmodelled passive tissue ([Rankin, Rubenson & Hutchinson 2016](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2016.0035)).

## Humans and robots buy robustness with distance from the edge

The control literature turns the lean into arithmetic. On flat ground the CoP is the same point as the zero-moment point (ZMP), and it can only lie inside the support polygon. When it reaches the polygon's edge, the foot is about to rotate ([Vukobratović & Borovac 2004](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219843604000083); [Goswami 1999](https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649922066376)). Hof's extrapolated centre of mass is XcoM = x + v/ω0, where x is the CoM position, v its velocity and ω0 = √(g/l). It must stay inside the base of support, and the **margin of stability b is its distance to the nearest edge** ([Hof, Gazendam & Sinke 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.03.025)). A change in CoM velocity Δv can be absorbed by moving the CoP by Δv/ω0 ([Hof 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2007.08.003)). The XcoM is the same quantity as the capture point, and a state can be recovered without stepping when that point lies inside the region the CoP can reach ([Pratt et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHR.2006.321385); [Koolen et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364912452673)).

Applied to the main-branch model, these formulas give concrete numbers. The model's COM sits about 0.88 m above the floor in the home pose, so ω0 ≈ 3.3 s⁻¹, and its pad half-length is 0.07 m. Counting the pad alone, the largest forward velocity disturbance it can absorb without stepping is about **0.23 m/s with the CoP at the pad centre, 0.12 m/s at a pad statistic of 0.5, 0.047 m/s at the 0.80 gate bar and zero at the drifted stance of about 1.0**. These are my arithmetic and ignore the extra support the passive digits give beyond the pad edge. That explains why a joint-space action-noise test breaks these policies. It also explains why r10 made things worse. Lengthening the load-bearing support moves the edge, but robustness is the distance from the operating point to the edge, and a policy that nothing pulls back toward the middle drifts to the new edge with the same near-zero margin.

Healthy people keep that distance large on purpose. In quiet stance the mean CoP sits at **45.7% of foot length from the heel** ([Fujiwara et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.2003.96.2.381)). Individual means range from 31% to 58%, and people can hold targets anywhere from 20% to 80% ([Fujiwara et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.2114/jpa2.29.197)). The range people use voluntarily is only about 60% of foot length in young adults, and 40% or less in the elderly ([Schieppati et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0); [King, Judge & Wolfson 1994](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronj/49.6.m258)). Leaning away from the middle increases sway area, calf (soleus) activity and toe-flexor recruitment ([Schieppati et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0)), and it makes the CoP more variable ([Duarte & Zatsiorsky 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-002-1154-1)). People standing at the edge of an 81 cm drop move their CoP and CoM **backward, away from the edge**, and stiffen by 27.5% ([Carpenter et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/s002210100681)). Range also limits strategy: shifting the CoP with the ankle moves it sideways by no more than 2 cm, while a step reaches 20 cm ([Hof, Vermerris & Gjaltema 2010](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.042572)). These are fractions of a human foot measured from the heel. They cannot be compared with the project's pad statistic until they are converted (see the frame table below).

Humans also balance without using the foot. For small, slow disturbances on full-length support they use an ankle strategy, shifting the CoP with ankle torque. When the support is short or the disturbance fast, they recruit a hip strategy that produces a horizontal shear force with little ankle torque ([Horak & Nashner 1986](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1986.55.6.1369)). The hip strategy needs less muscle activation per unit of CoM acceleration ([Kuo & Zajac 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-6123(08)62294-3)), the two strategies form a continuum ([Runge et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0966-6362(99)00032-6)), and quiet stance is dominated by the ankle ([Gatev et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.1999.915ad.x)). Hof's equations give three ways to accelerate the CoM: move the CoP, counter-rotate body segments about the CoM, or apply an external force ([Hof 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2005.12.016)). A long, heavy tail makes the second channel strong. Lizards swing their tails to redirect angular momentum, and a modelled Velociraptor tail was more effective at this than an agama lizard's ([Libby et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10710)). The human 1:3 ratio of ankle to hip movement should not be copied onto a horizontal trunk-and-tail beam, but the principle carries over: a policy that corrects forward drift only with ankle and toe torque is using its most range-limited channel. On the main-branch model the tail pitch actuators span only about ±0.21 rad (±12°), with gains of 60–100 ([model file](../../../../environments/tyrannosaurus_rex/anatomical/assets/tyrannosaurus_rex_anatomical.xml)), so this channel's authority should be measured before anyone relies on it.

In humans, passive stiffness cannot hold an inverted pendulum by itself, and that limits what a physics change can do. The benchmark is the toppling stiffness m·g·h, where h is the height of the COM above the pivot. Measured against it, intrinsic human ankle stiffness is **91 ± 23% for tiny perturbations and 64 ± 8% for 1° perturbations, falling to 13–19% over larger rotations** ([Loram & Lakie 2002](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2002.025049); [Casadio, Morasso & Sanguineti 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2004.05.005); [Loram, Maganaris & Lakie 2007](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.140046)). Passive calf muscles alone can hold the body only at a COM-to-ankle angle of about 1.6°. Model fits put total effective feedback stiffness at about 1.33 × mgh, with damping that must rise with stiffness to avoid resonance ([Peterka 2002](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2002.88.3.1097)). Too much corrective torque produced a 1 Hz body oscillation ([Peterka & Loughlin 2004](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00516.2003)), and the real controller relies heavily on velocity information ([Masani et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00730.2002)). Winter's group argued that stiffness alone suffices ([Winter et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2001.85.6.2630)), but the direct measurements support Morasso's view that it does not ([Morasso & Schieppati 1999](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1999.82.3.1622)). The ranges of stable feedback gains at different stance widths do not even overlap ([Bingham, Choi & Ting 2011](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00010.2011)). A policy whose posture drifts mid-training therefore carries gains tuned for an operating point it has already left. On a humanoid, series elasticity at the ankle low-pass filtered the ZMP dynamics and steadied push recovery ([Eljaik et al. 2013](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss09/p08.html)).

The main-branch model's own numbers fit this picture. Its mgh is about **740 N·m/rad**. The six passive digit springs (40 N·m/rad each) sum to about 240 N·m/rad, roughly a third of that. The two ankle position servos (kp 900 each) sum to about 1,800 N·m/rad, about 2.4 × mgh (my arithmetic from the [model file](../../../../environments/tyrannosaurus_rex/anatomical/assets/tyrannosaurus_rex_anatomical.xml)). Joint-space action noise therefore reaches posture and the CoP almost unfiltered.

The sole tells humans where their load sits. Vibrating receptors under the forefoot or heel tilts people away from the simulated pressure, so the sole works as a "dynamometric map" of load position ([Kavounoudias, Roll & Roll 1998](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001756-199810050-00021)). Numbing the soles barely changes quiet sway: CoP velocity with eyes closed rises by only 11–12% ([Meyer, Oddsson & De Luca 2004a](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-003-1804-y)). It does shift responses to perturbations toward the hip ([Meyer, Oddsson & De Luca 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-004-1868-3)). Cooling the sole made people reproduce mid-foot target positions **further forward** ([Fujiwara et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.2003.96.2.381)), and cooling the heel blurred their sense of the rear limit ([Asai & Inaoka 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neulet.2021.135752)). Combined sensory loss from diabetic neuropathy increases instability by 66–117% ([Simoneau et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.17.12.1411)). Robots get the same information from force/torque sensors at the ankle and run foot-force control loops on it ([Kaneko et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.2004.1307969); [Kajita et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2010.5651082)).

The project's policy is in the position of a person with numb soles. The code sums each foot's pad and digit touch sensors into a single channel per foot ([observation code](../../../../environments/tyrannosaurus_rex/base_env.py)), so the policy receives each foot's total load. It also sees the passive toe-joint angles, which bend under digit load. But nothing in its input changes much as the CoP slides from the middle of the pad toward the front, so nothing pushes back against slow forward drift.

## Standard RL setups neither see nor price fore-aft weight placement

### Deployed policies hide contact from the actor but feed it to the critic and the reward

Hardware locomotion policies mostly keep foot force out of the deployed actor, the network that runs on the robot. ANYmal's student policy uses no foot contact states, while its teacher's privileged inputs are mostly about foot-ground interaction ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251)). Cassie's stair-climbing policy observes no foot force and uses foot forces only in its reward ([Siekmann et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.08328)). Humanoid-Gym and DBHL give foot contact to the critic (the value network used only in training) and not to the actor ([Humanoid-Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05695); [DBHL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17219)). Where contact sensing is cheap, as on the Unitree A1, RMA feeds the policy binary contact flags ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)).

Direct tests of contact inputs are scarce. On Mini Cheetah, a learned contact-probability input barely changed final reward (4.7212 with it, 4.7125 without), but it "stabilizes and accelerates learning" ([Ji et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05481)). The only clean with/without comparison is a simulation preprint using a linear policy ([Mhaske & Mandhare 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01102)). Policies without contact inputs rely on history instead. On ANYmal, a long proprioceptive history cut lateral deviation under a push by 35.5% ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251)), and on Cassie LSTM policies far outperformed feedforward ones on stairs ([Siekmann et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.08328)). A memoryless SB3 MLP has neither privileged contact information nor history, so it can infer fore-aft load only from proxies such as joint angles. **No peer-reviewed, hardware-validated ablation of CoP or foot force as a policy input for standing exists.**

### A left-right mirror cannot see a fore-aft lean

Mirror-symmetry methods improve left-right symmetry and motion quality, but not reliable learning, and they do nothing for fore-aft posture. Yu and colleagues put the mirror loss in the policy update because a reward term would break the policy-gradient assumptions ([Yu, Turk & Liu 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.08093)). A later re-analysis found that the loss helped only alongside Yu's curriculum, hurt the humanoid without it, and had "no consistent and predictable impact" on learning speed ([Abdolhosseini et al. 2019](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2019-MIG-symmetry/2019-MIG-symmetry.pdf)). On ANYmal, mirrored data augmentation trained fastest. The loss competed with the RL gradient, pushing toward "equivalent actions for all equivalent states without considering the respective rewards" ([Mittal et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04359)). Both papers flag a neutral-pose trap: a strictly symmetric policy cannot break symmetry to take a first step, and the documented fix is random joint noise on the initial pose.

**A left-right reflection maps a symmetric forward lean onto itself.** Arm L's KL mirror loss (κ = 0.3) therefore has no gradient against the lean by construction, which matches the project's result. Yu's weight w = 4 multiplies an L2 action loss, so it is not comparable with arm L's KL κ.

### Exploration noise that shrinks lets stances drift toward the edge

The policy's exploration noise doubles as robustness training. Cassie's designers fixed the action standard deviation at about 0.1 rad, because constantly injected noise makes the policy "adapt itself to handle unmodeled disturbances", and they tested robustness by adding noise to the actions ([Xie et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09537)). Training against adversarial perturbations added to the actions also improved performance without perturbations ([Tessler, Efroni & Mannor 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09184)). Maximum-entropy RL maximises a lower bound on a robust objective ([Eysenbach & Levine 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06257)).

SB3's defaults point the other way. PPO starts with log_std 0, a standard deviation of 1.0 in action units ([SB3 policies.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/policies.py)), against a best initial value of about 0.5 in a large study of on-policy RL ([Andrychowicz et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05990)). That noise is free to shrink during training, and SB3 evaluates deterministically by default ([SB3 evaluation.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/evaluation.py)). A policy that needs precise actions to balance at the pad edge can therefore look fine in evaluation and still fail an action-noise test. Training a stochastic policy and deploying its deterministic version is a recognised trade-off ([Montenegro et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02235)). gSDE gives smoother exploration ([Raffin, Kober & Stulp 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05719)). Randomising every physics parameter buys conservatism: a Laikago quadruped trotted at 0.9 m/s when trained with dynamics randomisation and 1.1 m/s without ([Xie et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02404)).

Most relevant to the project's rule: on Cassie, **standing-only policies, whether or not they were trained with pushes, kept falling when forced to lean past the support region. A policy trained on walking and standing stepped and then returned to standing** ([Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16889)). No paper shows a noise schedule moving a standing robot's CoP toward the edge, so that link is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

### Benchmark stand rewards pay nothing for placement

Benchmark stand rewards pay for head height, uprightness, small control effort and low CoM velocity, and nothing for where the weight sits on the feet ([dm_control humanoid.py](https://github.com/google-deepmind/dm_control/blob/main/dm_control/suite/humanoid.py)). Balance work that cared about placement added it explicitly. Yang and colleagues rewarded keeping the CoM over the centre of the foot ([Yang, Komura & Li 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02074)). On iCub, Ferigo and colleagues gave a bonus for keeping the CoM inside a support polygon shrunk by 2.5 cm, and a reward for keeping each foot's CoP at the centre of its sole ([Ferigo et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14534)). On the Unitree H1-2, a term pulling the ZMP toward the centre of the support polygon raised success rates in most MuJoCo settings tested ([DBHL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17219)). Ferigo's policy first converged on **feet tipping off flat contact** and needed an extra foot-orientation term. That is the closest published analogue to the T. rex toe lean.

Other emergent-posture failures follow the same pattern: behaviour the reward never priced. OstrichRL bent its toes back. Learning-to-Run agents never bent one knee until a knee term was added ([Learning to Run solutions](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00361)). A dog policy escaped fall detection by jittering ([Peng & van de Panne 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01055)). A termination threshold on orientation, such as RMA's 0.2 rad pitch limit, leaves every smaller lean free ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)). No ablation isolates a CoP-centring term in standing; DBHL's ablation is on locomotion. Under the project's 2026-10-16 rule, these terms are options for the maintainer, not next steps.

### Reward-picked checkpoints and three seeds prove little

Reward-based selection is the wrong tool for this failure. Henderson and colleagues ran identical TRPO configurations, split the runs into two groups of five seeds, and got statistically different results ([Henderson et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560)). Reporting the best score seen during training shifts comparisons by more than the effects being studied ([Agarwal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)). Picking the best of many noisy estimates biases the result upward, so a two-stage design is recommended: select, then re-evaluate on fresh runs ([Patterson et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01315)). SB3's EvalCallback does the biased version, with deterministic rollouts ([SB3 callbacks.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/callbacks.py)).

Because the reward does not fall as the lean develops, early checkpoints win partly by noise. They may also be genuinely better centred, since they predate the drift at 2–2.5M steps, which is a reason to choose them deliberately on a centring metric. Reliability metrics that separate drawdown within a run from variability across runs and across rollouts, with CVaR to measure risk, fit this job ([Chan et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05663)). One clean seed in three is ordinary deep-RL variance: DEP-RL's MPO baseline reached a symmetric gait in 1 of 5 seeds ([DEP-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00484)). With three seeds per arm, 3/3 clean against 1/3 gives a two-sided Fisher exact p of 0.40. With eight seeds per arm, 8/8 against 3/8 gives p = 0.026 (my arithmetic).

## One frame for every number puts the drift ahead of both analogues

Two research notes compared numbers that do not share a frame, and those comparisons need correcting before any audit. The anatomy note set the model's r9 figures (0.76–0.79) and r10 figures (0.89–0.94) directly against a band of 0.3–0.55 "of track length from the rear". The balance note proposed a target of 40–55% of contact length. The first comparison has three problems.

First, the project's statistic is the absolute fore-aft CoP offset from the pad centre divided by the pad's half-length, so 0.80 already means about 0.90 of pad length from the rear. Second, the r9 and r10 figures are the furthest forward leans that at least 5 of 8 spawns survive in a diagnostic (single episodes reached 0.85 and 1.10). They are not where the policy stands; the drifting seeds actually stood near 1.0. Third, the 0.3–0.55 band is the researcher's unpublished working hypothesis, not a measured value.

## Conclusion

The forward lean is a margin problem that looks like an anatomy problem. Anatomy supplies the target frame and tells the project where the tipping edge should sit, but it cannot supply the cure, because any policy that is neither paid for centring nor able to sense the fore-aft load will drift to whatever edge it is given. One finding may surprise the maintainer: in footprint terms, the 0.80 gate bar already accepts stances forward of the human quiet-stance analogue and far forward of the bird one. A target closer to the pad centre, roughly where the track evidence places tyrannosaur load, has anatomical support, though moving the gate needs approval.

The literature runs out exactly where the project sits. There is no T. rex ankle or toe range of motion, no ratite standing pressure map, and no standing-RL ablation of a CoP term. The project's own margin telemetry and a properly powered seed comparison will therefore be the strongest evidence available. Among the published results, Li et al.'s Cassie finding, that stepping rescued leans which killed every stance-only policy, is the clearest reason to expect the walking pilot to say something about stance.

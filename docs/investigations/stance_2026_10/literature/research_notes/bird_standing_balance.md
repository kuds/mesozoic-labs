# Avian standing balance and foot mechanics: passive support, foot loading, posture and sensing in birds as theropod analogues

Scope note: these notes cover living birds, the closest analogues of theropods. Evidence comes mostly from a few species: flamingo, ostrich, emu, helmeted guinea fowl, chicken, pigeon and quail. Several key results come from 1 to 3 animals. Where a statement rests on an abstract or a secondary summary rather than the full paper, it says so. Values are reported as the source gave them.

## 1. Passive standing support: intertarsal (ankle) stability, "stay" mechanisms, tendon locking, ligaments; how much standing is passive

### Takeaway
Birds have real passive support mechanisms, but each one works only in one direction and only in a particular posture. The flamingo's proximal "gravitational stay apparatus" carries body weight on one leg without muscle, but not on two legs. The ostrich intertarsal joint has a ligament-and-cam "engage-disengage mechanism" (EDM) with a snap-through point near 115°. Its passive moment is small compared with active ankle moments. The digital tendon-locking mechanism (TLM) is a perching and gripping feature that is weakly developed in ground birds, and its passivity is disputed. No study has measured what fraction of quiet standing in a large ground bird is passive.

### Cited Findings
**Flamingo one-legged standing (Chang & Ting 2017, Biol. Lett. 13:20160948)**
- Two fresh-frozen adult Caribbean flamingo cadavers (*Phoenicopterus ruber*) supported body weight on one leg with no muscle activity, in a stable posture resembling live birds. The joints stayed fixed while the tibiotarsus was tilted through 119° in the sagittal plane. Hip angle was 13.9 ± 5.7° and knee angle 104.5 ± 5.4°. — [Chang & Ting 2017, doi:10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948) ([PMC5454233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233))
- The mechanism was one-directional. The knee resisted extension but not flexion. A downward load in front of the knee moved the joints less than 10°. A downward load behind the body, near the tail, pitched the body backward, so it was not resisted. (The paper's reported force magnitude, "approx. 5–10° N", appears to be a typo, so the magnitude is unclear.) — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)
- The cadaver stayed stable with the tibiotarsus adducted 20° from vertical in the frontal plane. In an approximated two-legged posture (vertical alignment), the joints "rapidly became unstable and could no longer support the body weight." — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)
- The authors propose a passive, one-way "gravitational stay apparatus" in the proximal limb (hip and knee). It engages only under three conditions: (1) the body's centre of mass (CoM) is anterior to the knee, which produces passive knee-extensor and hip-flexor moments; (2) anatomical range-of-motion limits oppose those moments; (3) the foot is placed medial to the hip in the frontal plane. If any condition fails, the limb behaves as a free three-link chain. The responsible tissues (bony and cartilaginous hip and knee structures) are suggested, not confirmed. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233); Chang, as quoted by Emory, describes it as more like a hammock or sling than a lock — [Emory news](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2017/05/ting_flamingoes/index.html)
- The authors could not exclude high tonic muscle co-activation in live birds; they say EMG is needed. Their energy-saving explanation for one-legged standing is a hypothesis without direct evidence. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)

**Ostrich intertarsal joint "engage-disengage mechanism" (Schaller et al. 2009, J. Anat. 214:830–847)**
- Structures: the medial ligament LCML catches on the protruding medial epicondyle, which is the main source of the effect. The tendinous m. fibularis brevis catches on the distal lateral condylar crest, working with the lateral collateral ligament. The collateral ligaments clamp the joint surfaces closed. A bony process on the medial tibiotarsal condyle stops hyperextension. — [Schaller et al. 2009, doi:10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x) ([PMC2705294](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/))
- The transition point is about 115°, within a transition zone of roughly 125–105°. The fully extended (engaged) position is about 168°. During ground contact the joint never flexed below about 164° in walking or about 160° in running. In one fast run, mid-stance flexion reached about 140°. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)
- Passive moments measured in dissected limbs from 3 adult birds:

  | Limb condition | Resistance to flexion | Resistance to extension | Notes |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Unloaded, horizontal | Peak ≈ 8 N·m at 140°, falling to 0 at ≈ 115° | Peak ≈ 5 N·m at 95° | |
  | Axially loaded, vertical (standing position) | Peak ≈ 16 N·m at 140° | ≈ 1.5 N·m at full extension | Transition still at 115° |

  Resistance fell about 30% after one night of storage at 15 °C. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)
- Qualitative observation only: a pelvis-and-limb preparation stripped of all muscles and tendons, with only bones and ligaments left, "could stand upright when balanced sideways by hand." The authors hypothesize, without measuring it, that the m. fibularis longus tendon adds to EDM stability in vivo. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)
- Live kinematics came from 3 birds (two females of 90 kg, one male of 140 kg). Dissection used 3 freshly slaughtered adults. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)
- A later ostrich toe-kinematics paper restates (from the abstract) that the intertarsal joint stays engaged during stance to support body mass. — [Zhang et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e2857](https://peerj.com/articles/2857)

**Emu/ratite version of the mechanism**
- Ito, Hida, Kinugasa and colleagues (J. Robotics & Mechatronics) dissected an emu hindlimb. They then built a physical replica from 3D-printed ostrich bones, coil springs and nylon strings. They describe one muscle, three ligaments and the tibiotarsal articular surface producing two stable equilibria, near full extension and near full flexion, with the articular surface acting "like an engineering cam." The medial ligaments and articular-surface shape were key. These details come from the abstract only, and the volume number differs between listings. — [Fuji Press JRM listing](https://www.fujipress.jp/?p=14908)

**Digital tendon-locking mechanism (TLM) and passive toe flexion**
- Quinn & Baumel (1990) described the avian digital TLM: tubercles on the digital flexor tendons engage ridges (plicae) on the inner surface of the tendon sheath when the toes flex, holding the toes closed. Citation: Zoomorphology 109:281–293, doi:10.1007/BF00312195. The DOI was confirmed only through reference lists, and one web source gives the year wrongly as 2004. — [Einoder & Richardson 2007 reference list](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/ref/10.1071/MU06019); [Raptor Resource explainer](https://www.raptorresource.org/?p=24573)
- Einoder & Richardson (2007, *Emu*): owl families differ in how strongly the TLM is expressed and where it sits along the digit. Strigidae resemble Falconiformes; Tytonidae differ. — [Einoder & Richardson 2007, doi:10.1071/MU06019](https://www.publish.csiro.au/MU/MU06019)
- Einoder & Richardson (2006, *Ibis*) compared 12 raptor and 3 non-raptor species. Raptors have a distally located TLM, densely packed with taller, more robust locking elements, and falcons and owls converged on this design. — [UTas record](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/An_ecomorphological_study_of_the_raptorial_digital_tendon_locking_mechanism/22857167/1)
- In a ground bird (the domestic chicken), flexor-tendon tubercles and sheath ridges are less developed than in parrots, and the chicken tendons contain chondrogenic (cartilage-like) elements. The authors suggest this reflects higher tendon loading in heavier ground birds, but only as a possibility. Passive toe flexion is said to have two components: the automatic digital flexor mechanism and the TLM. — [Trbojević Vukičević et al. 2018, Vet. Arhiv 88:125–138, doi:10.24099/vet.arhiv.170511](https://doi.org/10.24099/vet.arhiv.170511)
- Whether the TLM is passive is disputed. Galton & Shepherd (2012, J. Exp. Zool. A 317:205–215) reported that the automatic perching mechanism does not explain perching in European starlings. This was seen only as a citation and in secondary summaries; the full text was not read. — [cited in arXiv:2303.17057](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.17057); [ScienceABC summary (secondary)](https://www.scienceabc.com/nature/animals/why-dont-birds-fall-off-branches-when-they-sleep.html)
- A 2014 student study of window-strike carcasses supported a perching mechanism with both passive and active elements. This is not peer-reviewed. — [Butler University URC 2014](https://digitalcommons.butler.edu/urc/2014/biology/58)

**Hip passive structures**
- In the ostrich OpenSim model, the hip ad/abduction reserve actuator supplied more than 50% of that joint moment in all simulations. The authors interpret it as standing in for unmodelled passive tissues, including bony stops such as the antitrochanter and ligaments. — [Rankin, Rubenson & Hutchinson 2016, doi:10.1098/rsif.2016.0035](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2016.0035) ([PMC4892259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892259))

### Inferences
- The flamingo and ostrich mechanisms share a pattern. Each is a one-way stop that engages only when the load pushes the joint against its limit: the CoM in front of the knee, or the intertarsal joint near full extension. A simulated equivalent would be asymmetric joint limits, or one-sided springs and dampers, that resist collapse in one direction only. It would not be symmetric stiffness.
- Rough scaling from the numbers above: the ostrich's peak passive EDM moment of about 16 N·m, divided by about 90 kg × 9.81 m/s² × about 1.1 m leg length, gives roughly 0.015 in units of body weight × leg length. Emus rising from sitting need peak ankle extensor moments of 0.16–0.22 in the same units (Lin et al. 2024, Section 2). The passive intertarsal moment is therefore roughly an order of magnitude smaller than active ankle demands during movement. It is better read as a posture detent, or "snap", that holds the joint in its engaged range, than as a weight-bearing support. Quiet standing could still be largely passive if the ground reaction force passes close to the ankle. The 1.1 m leg length is an assumed value, and this is not a measurement.
- The flamingo result implies passive stays depend on posture. A model that drifts out of the "engaged" posture, for example by leaning forward onto the toes, would lose the passive support that a stay mechanism provides.
- The TLM is unlikely to be relevant to the T. rex: it is a gripping feature, poorly developed in ground birds, and its passivity is disputed. Digital flexor tendons in ground birds act mainly as elastic springs during locomotion (see Section 5).

### Gaps
- No EMG-backed measurement exists of how much quiet two-legged standing is passive in any large ground bird (ostrich, emu, rhea). The flamingo study covered one-legged standing in a wading bird, with n = 2 cadavers.
- The emu physical-model paper's equilibrium angles and stiffness values could not be read; only the abstract was available.
- Quinn & Baumel (1990) could not be read, so it is unconfirmed which groups (for example ratites) lack a TLM. No source was found describing a TLM in ostrich, emu or rhea.
- No quantitative passive moment-angle data were found for the avian metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, beyond the qualitative "held elevated by ligaments" (Section 2).

## 2. Foot mechanics in large ground birds: ostrich two-toed foot, emu/rhea three-toed feet, MTP joint and plantar pads, ground reaction force and centre of pressure, claws and digit III

### Takeaway
In the ostrich, digit III carries most of the load. In running it took about 77% of vertical force on solid ground and about 84% on sand, with digit IV at 14–23%. Digit IV acts as a lateral stabiliser, or "outrigger", and the digit III claw works as a positional anchor at push-off. The MTP joint is held permanently elevated ("supra-jointed") by ligaments. During locomotion the centre of pressure (CoP) follows a J-shaped path from the lateral digit III toward the claw. No standing pressure data and no emu or rhea pressure data were found.

### Cited Findings
**Ostrich (*Struthio camelus*) foot**
- Each foot has two digits, III and IV. Digit III has two roughly parallel digital cushions (pads) and digit IV has one. The MTP joint is "permanently elevated" and stores elastic energy in muscle-tendon units. The claw of digit III is a third functional part. — [Zhang et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e3613, doi:10.7717/peerj.3613](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3613) ([PMC5530993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/))
- The MTP joint stays above the ground throughout loading (a "supra-jointed" posture), and it remained elevated after all muscles and tendons were removed. The authors therefore attribute the elevation mainly to ligaments. The MTP joint extends further as running speed increases. The toe flexor tendons are proposed as the main site of elastic energy storage, with the engaged intertarsal joint supporting that role. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705294/)
- Schaller et al. (2011, J. Exp. Biol. 214:1123–1130, doi:10.1242/jeb.043596) used pedobarography (pressure-plate measurement) on hand-raised ostriches. From the abstract: load was distributed "rather inconsistently" during walking, while every running trial left a tripod-like toe-print. Walking and running shared a J-shaped CoP trajectory, and its origin became more localised as speed increased. — [Univ. Antwerp abstract](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/88787); [Schaller PhD thesis, Heidelberg](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/8852)
- Thesis-level summary: digit IV mainly provides auxiliary balance, which makes its evolutionary loss unlikely. The highly directed impact of the digit III claw tip gives significant grip and acts as a "positional anchor" during running. — [Schaller thesis](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/8852); summarised in [Zhang et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/) as "3rd toe bears most of the body load, the 4th toe is responsible for stabilization, and the claw acts as a position anchor."
- Running loads (Zhang et al. 2017; loads in multiples of body weight, BW):

  | Measure | Loose sand | Solid ground |
  |---|---|---|
  | Total vertical force, running | > 2.5 BW | > 1.8 BW |
  | Digit III share | ≈ 84% | ≈ 77% |
  | Digit IV share | ≈ 14% | ≈ 23% |
  | Claw share at toe-off | 1.5% | 2.4% |
  | Peak total force, walking | 1.3 BW | 1.1 BW |

  Running peak pressures on sand, normalised to BW, were about 2.6–3.0 in total, 2.2–2.3 under digit III, about 0.4 under digit IV and 0.02–0.04 under the claw. — [Zhang et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/)
- CoP path: on solid ground in running, the CoP was J-shaped, starting at the lateral digit III and moving to the claw after about 70% of stance. On loose sand it mostly started from the middle of digit III and moved toward the claw. Walking on sand was more variable. — [Zhang et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/)
- A PeerJ preprint (abstract level) states that digit III sustains most of the ground reaction force (GRF) and its claw provides the push-off forces in fast locomotion. — [PeerJ preprint 2324](https://peerj.com/preprints/2324.pdf)

**Emu (*Dromaius novaehollandiae*), three-toed foot, standing up**
- In emus rising from sitting (sit-to-stand), the CoP stays within the base of support formed by the tarsometatarsus and digits. Foot placement changes how close the CoM lies to the caudal (rear) edge of the third TMP joint. At heel-off the CoM has moved forward and sits behind the toes. — [Lin et al. 2024, J. Exp. Biol. 227:jeb247519, doi:10.1242/jeb.247519](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519) ([PMC11708823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11708823/))

**Flamingo (one foot, quiet stance)**
- In quiescent one-legged standing, the CoP sat directly under the tarsometatarsophalangeal (TMP; equivalent to MTP) joint, implying little or no net torque about that joint. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)

### Inferences
- Across the flamingo (quiet stance) and emu (rising), birds keep the CoP near the MTP/TMP joint and the proximal digit pads, not out at the toe tips. In locomotion the CoP moves toward the claw only late in stance, at push-off (ostrich, after about 70% of stance). A standing T. rex policy that settles with its weight on the toe tips matches the bird push-off phase, not bird quiet stance.
- Digit III is the main load path; the lateral digit (IV), and by extension digits II and IV in three-toed feet, stabilises against roll. This supports a foot with a dominant central load-bearing digit and lateral digits that add mediolateral support.
- An elevated MTP held by ligaments implies the foot "heel" (the distal tarsometatarsus) does not normally bear weight in large ground birds. The weight-bearing surface is the digital pads under and just in front of the MTP joint.

### Gaps
- No pressure-plate or CoP data were found for quiet two-legged standing in ostrich, emu or rhea. All ostrich pressure data are from walking and running.
- No emu or rhea plantar pressure or toe-load distribution study was found, and no standing-specific digit II/III/IV load shares for any three-toed ground bird.
- The full text of Schaller et al. 2011 was not accessed, so its per-toe load percentages and walking-trial details are unverified here.
- No mechanical properties (stiffness, damping) of avian plantar digital pads under load were found.

## 3. Posture and centre of mass: CoM relative to the foot, crouched posture, perturbation responses, postural sway

### Takeaway
In standing birds the CoM lies anterior to the hip (pelvic girdle) and vertically over the middle of the foot. The bird places the foot under the CoM mainly by keeping the femur near-horizontal and flexed at the hip, and by setting knee and ankle angles. If the CoM is moved, birds re-pose the femur rather than lean. The only quantitative quiet-standing sway data are from flamingos on one leg: a CoP 95% radius of 3.2 mm (5–10% of foot length) when quiescent. Perturbation studies of birds are mostly of running (guinea fowl) or of standing on moving perches (pigeons, hens). They point to limb posture and the intrinsic mechanics of distal muscles, plus trunk rotation and wing flapping. No study frames bird responses as human-style "hip versus ankle" strategies.

### Cited Findings
**Where the CoM sits**
- In the domestic chicken the CoM is "located anterior to the pelvic girdle and vertically aligned with the feet when standing." The paper cites Grossi et al. 2014 and Carrano & Biewener 1999 (J. Morphol. 240:237–249) for this. — [LeBlanc et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11:e0153477, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0153477](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153477)
- Grossi et al. 2014 assumed the CoM in normal chickens is vertically aligned with the middle of the foot during standing. They describe typical birds as having a highly flexed hip, a near-horizontal femur and feet placed in front of the hip, and say that at mid-stance "the feet must sit under the CoM." — [Grossi et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9:e88458, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0088458](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0088458)
- Chickens fitted with artificial tails (15% of body mass; a posterior CoM shift calculated at about 2 cm, not measured) responded by re-posing the limb rather than leaning:

  | Measure | Artificial tail | Controls |
  |---|---|---|
  | Femur orientation, standing | ≈ 40% more vertical | — |
  | Femoral range of motion in stance, walking | 43.7° | 15.4° |
  | Knee angle at end of stance | 102.0° | 83.3° |
  | Ankle angle at foot-down | 138.7° | 128.8° |
  | Ankle angle at lift-off | 152.4° | 136.0° |

  The tailed birds also stood with the ankle more flexed and the tibiotarsus more horizontal, and walked in a more "hip-driven" way. The authors liken this to crocodilians, mammals and, hypothetically, non-avian theropods. — [Grossi et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0088458)
- Across bird-line archosaurs (17 taxa reconstructed in 3D), Allen et al. (2013) inferred a gradual, stepwise shift toward a more crouched hindlimb posture. The shift accelerated within Maniraptora, and forelimb enlargement had more effect on CoM position than tail reduction. — [Allen et al. 2013, Nature, doi:10.1038/nature12059](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12059); [Dryad data record](https://datadryad.org/stash/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.hh74n)
- Macaulay et al. (2023) found CoM position grouped by lifestyle (terrestrial versus flying) rather than lineage, and that limb proportions and CoM evolved modularly. They suggest fully crouched bipedalism evolved after powered flight. — [Macaulay et al. 2023, Nat. Commun. 14:1575, doi:10.1038/s41467-023-37317-y](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natcom/v14y2023i1d10.1038_s41467-023-37317-y.html); [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/id/eprint/3169005)
- In broiler chickens, pectoral muscle growth in the final weeks shifts the CoM forward and upward, which may increase limb muscle stress during standing and moving. — [Paxton et al. 2014, PeerJ, doi:10.7717/peerj.473](https://peerj.com/articles/473.pdf)
- In quail, a pronograde (horizontal) trunk requires asymmetric leg function in steady locomotion: a ground reaction force skewed to one side and a leg longer at touchdown than at lift-off. The authors argue stability depends on trunk orientation and CoM location, and that this constrains leg length, stiffness and damping. — [Andrada et al. 2014, Proc. R. Soc. B (PMC4240980)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4240980)
- Emus (about 30 kg) rising from sitting extend their joints in sequence from proximal to distal and keep a fairly horizontal, rigid trunk with little trunk flexion. They use a short "forward momentum" phase that starts with a small backward GRF. The authors suggest the emu's cranially placed CoM, together with passive mechanisms, supports stability. — [Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519)

**Postural sway (quiet standing)**
- Flamingos standing on one leg (8 juvenile *P. chilensis*, 0.25–1.40 kg):

  | Measure | Quiescent (eyes closed) | Active (eyes open, moving) |
  |---|---|---|
  | CoP 95% confidence radius | 3.2 ± 0.5 mm (5–10% of foot length) | 5.1 ± 0.4 mm |
  | Peak CoP velocity | 40 ± 19 mm/s | 296 ± 15 mm/s |
  | CoP diffusion | 1.7 ± 0.7 mm² | 4.5 ± 0.6 mm² |

  Awake but non-moving birds had a peak CoP velocity of 79 ± 1 mm/s. The authors model quiescent balance as an inverted pendulum needing little muscle activity. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454233)
- No other avian quiet-standing sway (posturography) study was found. Broiler force-plate work covers walking: in 25 market-age broilers, slow speed, a wide base of support, large lateral CoM movements, and mediolateral forces larger than fore-aft forces. — [Paxton et al. 2013, J. Exp. Biol. 216:3237, doi:10.1242/jeb.080309](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.080309)

**Perturbation responses**
- Pigeons standing on a perch moved sinusoidally by a motion platform, along 3 translational and 3 rotational axes:
  - The head followed translations but was almost perfectly stabilised against rotations.
  - The body compensated for roll and pitch rotations, but not for translations or yaw.
  - Vision improved head stabilisation only for translations.
  The paper gives no numbers in its abstract. — [Theunissen & Troje 2017, Front. Neurosci. 11:551, doi:10.3389/fnins.2017.00551](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00551)
- Laying hens on a swaying versus a static perch (30 s trials):

  | Measure | Swaying perch | Static perch |
  |---|---|---|
  | Falls | 0.4 ± 0.08 | 0.04 ± 0.02 |
  | Maximum angular velocity | 983.7 ± 8.6 °/s | 127 ± 18.3 °/s |
  | Maximum linear acceleration | 7.2 ± 0.1 m/s² | 1 ± 0.1 m/s² |

  Wing flapping is described as a muscular response to balance disturbance, possibly adding aerodynamic force to inertial torques. On perches, birds balance by actively contracting the toe flexors. — [LeBlanc et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153477)
- Guinea fowl running over an unexpected drop: limb contact angle explained most of the variation in limb loading; limb stiffness had little effect. Posture-dependent limb work let birds absorb energy and keep speed, an "inherent velocity control." — [Daley & Biewener 2006, PNAS (PMC1622881)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1622881)
- Proximo-distal gradient hypothesis (abstract-level):
  - Hip and knee muscles are mainly feedforward and insensitive to load.
  - Ankle and TMP muscles are load-sensitive and rely on intrinsic mechanics and fast proprioceptive feedback.
  - The hip kept the same role regardless of loading; the ankle and TMP switched between spring-like and damping roles depending on limb posture at contact.
  - Initial knee angle set limb posture.
  — [Daley, Felix & Biewener 2007, J. Exp. Biol. 210:383–394](https://biewenerlab.oeb.harvard.edu/publications/running-stability-enhanced-proximo-distal-gradient-joint-neuromechanical)
- Conflicting evidence: a later treadmill belt-speed perturbation study (7 guinea fowl) did not support a general proximo-distal gradient, because distal joint kinematics stayed largely unchanged. Distal joints did appear sensitive to changes in vertical loading, but not to fore-aft foot velocity. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10334289)

### Inferences
- Birds balance by placing the foot under a CoM that sits ahead of the hip, using a near-horizontal femur. When the CoM moves they change femur and ankle angles, not trunk lean. The analogue target for the project is to keep the CoM projection over the middle of the foot, near the MTP and proximal digit pads, as the reward or target posture. Grossi et al. suggest non-avian theropods, with a more posterior CoM because of the tail, would use a more vertical femur.
- Flamingo quiescent sway of about 3 mm radius (5–10% of foot length), with the CoP under the MTP joint, gives a rough target: a calm standing policy might keep CoP excursion within about 10% of foot length around the MTP region. This extrapolates from a small wading bird on one leg.
- The bird evidence favours limb posture (contact angle, knee angle) and intrinsically stable distal muscles over large, fast trunk "hip strategy" corrections. Pigeons do rotate the body against roll and pitch platform rotations, and wings act as a backup.

### Gaps
- No study was found measuring CoM position relative to the foot in standing ostriches, emus or rheas. No support-surface translation or tilt study of standing large ground birds was found, and no EMG of standing balance in ratites.
- No avian study was found that explicitly tests "hip versus ankle strategy" during standing.
- Tail use for standing balance in birds is not addressed; birds' tails are short. Evidence for trunk and tail use comes only from the chicken artificial-tail study.

## 4. Plantar and leg sensing: Herbst and Grandry corpuscles, plantar mechanoreception, lumbosacral organ, vestibular and visual contributions

### Takeaway
Bird legs contain vibration-sensitive Herbst corpuscles (Pacinian-like), plus nociceptors and other mechanoreceptors in the scaly foot and leg skin. No study shows birds use plantar pressure or CoP sensing for balance. The best balance-specific evidence points instead to two other sources. One is the lumbosacral organ (LSO), a putative accelerometer-like sensor in the pelvis next to the hip. The other is proprioceptive feedback from load-sensitive distal (ankle) muscles. Both are supported by limited or qualitative data.

### Cited Findings
**Leg and foot skin receptors**
- In the duck hind limb, 72 of 94 recorded units were highly phasic, vibration-sensitive mechanoreceptors in the interosseous region, interpreted as Herbst corpuscles. Their thresholds were similar to mammalian Pacinian corpuscles, but they had wider low-frequency cut-offs and could signal higher frequencies. Their fibres were about 5–10 μm in diameter. The authors suggest they act as a "warning device" detecting vibration of the ground or perch. — [Dorward & McIntyre 1971, J. Physiol. 219:77–87, doi:10.1113/jphysiol.1971.sp009650](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1971.sp009650) ([PMC1331618](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331618))
- Herbst corpuscles respond mainly to transient force and high-frequency vibration. Grandry corpuscles respond to changes in transient force, low-frequency vibration and velocity. In the bill, densities reach up to about 200 corpuscles/mm². This is a general summary, mostly from bill studies. — [Wikipedia summary (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpuscles_of_Herbst)
- A 1970 paper on "The distribution of Herbst corpuscles in the foot of the domestic fowl" exists (archive catalogue only; findings not accessed). A 1978 study of chicken toe skin focused on Grandry corpuscles (seen as a title only). — [Edinburgh archive entry](https://archives.collections.ed.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/51641)
- The scaly skin of the chicken lower leg has C-fibre mechanothermal nociceptors with receptive fields of about 1–3 mm. Mechanical thresholds were 0.8–15 g with von Frey filaments and 3–33 g with a 0.5 mm probe. — [Gentle & Tilston 2000, Animal Welfare](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-welfare/article/nociceptors-in-the-legs-of-poultry-implications-for-potential-pain-in-preslaughter-shackling/CA1B7F2CADCA7FA42ECEA7274BA790C7)
- Joint receptors: Ruffini and Herbst corpuscles occur in the articular capsule of the pigeon shoulder joint. This shows avian joint-capsule mechanoreceptors exist; the pigeon data are for the shoulder, not the leg. — [Halata & Munger 1980 record](https://fis.uke.de/portal/en/publications/the-ultrastructure-of-ruffini-and-herbst-corpuscles-in-the-articular-capsule-of-domestic-pigeon(7f999b64-2472-4bfe-a15b-6ea21659af86)/export.html)
- Human comparison only: vibrating plantar skin receptors shifts the standing CoP in a direction that depends on the foot region stimulated (the plantar sole as a "dynamometric map"). No avian equivalent was found. — [Kavounoudias et al. 1998, PubMed 9831459](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9831459/)

**Proprioception in distal leg muscles**
- Guinea fowl with a self-reinnervated lateral gastrocnemius (LG; the reinnervation removes its monosynaptic stretch reflex) were compared with intact birds while walking over obstacles (n = 6 per group):

  | Measure | Intact birds | Reinnervated birds |
  |---|---|---|
  | Rise in LG EMG, obstacle stride | 68% | 31%, then a further 43% in the next stride |
  | Rise in peak force, obstacle stride | 22% | 43% |
  | Rise in net work | 3.6 J/kg | 0.7 J/kg |
  | Recovery | Within 1 stride | Elevated force over 3 strides |

  Reinnervated birds had a more "strut-like" LG and a more crouched posture. — [Schwaner et al. 2023, J. Exp. Biol. 226:jeb245199, doi:10.1242/jeb.245199](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.245199)
- Daley et al. 2009, as summarised by Schwaner et al. 2023: after an unexpected drop, a posture change shortened the LG and cut its force by about 70% despite similar activation. Stability came from intrinsic mechanics without a reflex. — [Schwaner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.245199)

**Lumbosacral organ (LSO)**
- Anatomy:
  - The LSO sits in an enlarged lumbosacral spinal cord (segments about 27–38).
  - Bilateral mechanosensory "accessory lobe" neurons lie beside the spinal cord ligaments.
  - A dense glycogen body lies in the dorsally split cord.
  - Bony transverse canals surround the region.
  The organ is between the hips, near the CoM. — [Urbina-Meléndez et al. 2018, Front. Robot. AI 5:38, doi:10.3389/frobt.2018.00038](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00038)
- Behaviour: Necker et al. (2000) removed the dorsal synsacral bone over the LSO in pigeons. The birds "could fly but had difficulty perching or walking," and were worse when masked (deprived of vision). They tried to recover balance with flight-type wing reflexes. The results are qualitative and from few individuals. — [Stanchak et al. 2020, Integr. Org. Biol. 2:obaa024, doi:10.1093/iob/obaa024](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa024); citation list at [Necker's site](https://reinhold-necker.de/seite5.html) (Necker et al. 2000, J. Comp. Physiol. A 186:409–412; Necker 2006, J. Comp. Physiol. A 192:439–448)
- Ablating accessory-lobe neurons disrupts standing balance (Necker 2006, as cited). Birds still compensate reflexively for body rotations after labyrinthectomy and spinal transection. — [Urbina-Meléndez et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00038); [Kamska et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa037)
- Prevalence and status of the evidence:
  - Osteological signs of the LSO were found in all 44 species examined, but no ratites were sampled.
  - Necker (2006) reported deeper canals in the ostrich.
  - Terrestrial locomotion did not correlate clearly with LSO traits.
  - "All hypotheses of LSO function lack conclusive evidence," and "no specific transduction mechanism has been identified."
  — [Stanchak et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa024)
- Proposed mechanism in quail:
  - The LSO is a fluid-submerged mass-spring-damper. The glycogen body is the mass (13 mm³, density ≥ 1.40 g/cm³, effective force about 51 μN), the denticulate ligament network is the spring, and cerebrospinal fluid is the damper.
  - Estimated ligament strain is about 5% (upward) and 2% (downward) on average, with peaks of about 8% and 1%.
  - Left-right and front-back pairs of accessory lobes could allow sensing of roll and pitch.
  - Natural frequency was not estimated. Data come from one scanned quail and 3 dissections.
  — [Kamska, Daley & Badri-Spröwitz 2020, Integr. Org. Biol. 2:obaa037, doi:10.1093/iob/obaa037](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa037)
- Physical model: an articulated aluminium guinea fowl skeleton (20 cm hip height, rubber-band "muscles", toes fixed to a platform moved by a 6-DOF robot arm).
  - Foot-to-hip sensing delay was a median 0.02 s, versus 0.095 s foot-to-head with a low-stiffness neck (0.03 s versus 0.055 s with a stiff neck).
  - Hip accelerometers explained 30.81–48.96% of variance in foot acceleration, versus 15.59–22.19% for head accelerometers.
  - Fusing hip and head signals did not improve on the hip alone.
  The authors suggest the LSO is used for body balance and the vestibular system for head and gaze stability, and recommend pelvis-located sensing for compliant bipedal robots. — [Urbina-Meléndez et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00038)

**Vestibular and visual**
- Pigeons stabilise the head against rotation almost perfectly, and vision helps mainly against translations. Head stabilisation is described as using eyes, vestibular system and neck and body proprioceptors. — [Theunissen & Troje 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00551)
- Masked pigeons with LSO lesions were worse than sighted ones, implying vision partly substitutes for the LSO in walking and perching balance. — [Stanchak et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/iob/obaa024)

### Inferences
- The project's observation set (joint angles and velocities plus torso orientation) resembles a bird's proprioceptive and vestibular inputs. Torso orientation is roughly the analogue of an LSO near the pelvis. The input birds have that the model lacks is load-sensitive proprioception from distal muscles (tendon force and stretch at the ankle and toes). Adding foot-load or ankle and MTP joint torque signals would be closer to avian sensing than adding a plantar "pressure map", for which there is no avian evidence.
- The Urbina-Meléndez result supports placing an accelerometer or IMU at the pelvis or hip, not the head, for estimating foot-ground disturbances on a robot.

### Gaps
- No study was found showing birds use plantar (foot-sole) mechanoreception or CoP sensing for standing balance. The foot Herbst-corpuscle distribution paper (1970) was not accessed.
- No study of plantar receptors or the LSO in ratites (ostrich, emu, rhea) was found.
- Necker's lesion studies are qualitative and from few birds, and no quantitative replication was found. The 2000 and 2006 papers were not read directly. Their DOIs (10.1007/s003590050440 and 10.1007/s00359-006-0105-x) come from memory and were not verified in this session.
- No Golgi tendon organ or muscle spindle density data for ratite leg muscles were retrieved.

## 5. Musculoskeletal ostrich and bird simulations relevant to standing

### Takeaway
The main ostrich simulations, the OpenSim model of Rankin et al. 2016 and the MuJoCo-based OstrichRL of La Barbera et al. 2021, target walking and running, not standing. Neither models ligaments or the intertarsal EDM explicitly. OstrichRL's observations include no foot contact force or CoM position. Its learned running policies showed a failure mode similar to the project's: toes plantarflexed and ankles held straight. Standing segments in its motion-tracking clips were among the slowest to learn. The emu sit-to-stand model (Lin et al. 2024) is the closest published simulation of a ratite getting to standing.

### Cited Findings
**OstrichRL (La Barbera et al. 2021)**
- Model: 120 muscles (68 leg, 52 neck) drive 50 joints (14 leg, 36 neck). Each leg has 7 hinges: 3 hip, 2 knee, 1 ankle and 1 MTP. Feet were initially flat and were reshaped to be more realistic. The model is based on CT scans and dissections. — [La Barbera et al. 2021, arXiv:2112.06061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.06061)
- Observations (running task): heights of head, pelvis and feet; joint positions and velocities; muscle forces, activations, lengths and velocities; forward CoM velocity. There are no foot contact forces or touch sensors and no CoM position.
- Rewards:
  - Running: forward CoM velocity.
  - Motion-capture tracking: a product of Gaussian kernels on CoM position and body orientation.
  — [La Barbera et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2112.06061)
- Passive properties: MuJoCo Hill-type muscles with an always-present passive force. Tendon slack and resting lengths are derived at compile time from length ranges. Activation and deactivation time constants are 10 and 40 ms. Neck joint stiffness was raised. Leg ligaments, joint damping and leg joint stiffness are not described. — [La Barbera et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2112.06061)
- Reported problems:
  - Muscle-driven running policies gave "unnatural" gaits, with toes plantarflexed and ankles straight, attributed to sparse rewards.
  - No dedicated standing task was trained; standing segments within motion-capture clips were among the slowest to learn.
  - MuJoCo was about 600× faster than OpenSim.
  — [La Barbera et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2112.06061)

**Rankin, Rubenson & Hutchinson 2016 (OpenSim ostrich)**
- Model: 34 musculotendon actuators. 8 degrees of freedom (DOF) per limb: hip 3, knee 3, ankle 1, MTP 1; the ankle and MTP were constrained to 1 DOF. The model includes digital flexors and extensors across the MTP. Data came from one 78.7 kg bird at 1.2 m/s (walking) and 3.5 m/s (running). — [Rankin et al. 2016, doi:10.1098/rsif.2016.0035](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2016.0035) ([PMC4892259](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892259))
- Biarticular hip-knee muscles were the main stance motors, and knee extensors acted as brakes. Digital flexors (FDL, FPPD3, FPD3, FPD4) acted as springs, absorbing energy early in stance and returning it late, and did more work in running. — [Rankin et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892259)
- Reserve torques (non-muscle torque actuators the solver adds where muscles fall short):
  - Ankle and MTP reserve actuators produced substantial positive work (up to 31.71 J, 24%).
  - Doubling digital flexor strength removed the need for the MTP reserve; doubling ankle flexor strength did not reduce the ankle reserve.
  - The hip ad/abduction reserve exceeded 50% of that joint moment, interpreted as unmodelled passive hip tissues.
  — [Rankin et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892259)
- A related 36-muscle ostrich pelvic-limb model (Hutchinson, Rankin, Rubenson, Rosenbluth, Siston & Delp, PeerJ 2015) studied how limb orientation affects muscle moment arms and capacity. Only the abstract-level description was seen. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/musculoskeletal-modelling-of-an-ostrich-struthio-camelus-pelvic-l/)

**Emu sit-to-stand (Lin, Rankin, Lamas, Moazen & Hutchinson 2024)**
- Data: 2 subadult males (about 27.4 and 28.5 kg); 3 sit-to-stand (STS) and 9 sit-to-walk (STW) trials analysed. The model has 37 actuators and was built from a 48.8 kg cadaver. — [Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519)
- Peak extensor moments, nondimensionalised by body weight × leg length:

  | Joint | Sit-to-stand | Sit-to-walk | Timing |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Hip | ≈ 0.12 | ≈ 0.15 | Just before heel-off |
  | Ankle (the largest demand) | ≈ 0.16 | ≈ 0.22 | Just after heel-off |

  Knee net moments were small, with flexor moments dominating. — [Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519)
- Ankle extensors produced the largest forces: GM peaked at about 4.3 BW (STS) and 5.5 BW (STW). Compliant tendons reduced peak activations and fibre excursions, especially distally. Ligaments and other passive tissues were not modelled. — [Lin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247519)

**Physical model for sensing**
- The guinea fowl physical model with hip and head accelerometers is summarised in Section 4. — [Urbina-Meléndez et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2018.00038)

### Inferences
- OstrichRL's "toes plantarflexed, ankle straight" pathology under sparse rewards, and its slow-to-learn standing segments, closely match the project's forward lean onto the toes. Both are muscle- or actuator-driven policies whose reward does not specify where the weight sits on the foot. This is an analogous failure in published bird-model reinforcement learning (RL), not a demonstrated cause.
- In both published ostrich models, distal joints (ankle and MTP) are where actuator capacity falls short, needing reserves or stronger digital flexors, and passive tissues are left out. Adding passive one-way intertarsal and MTP elements, plus hip ab/adduction stops, is a documented gap in these models, not only in the project's.
- In the emu, standing up is dominated by ankle extensor moment. This supports giving the T. rex ankle enough extensor capacity, or a passive extension stop, so that the policy does not substitute toe plantarflexion for ankle support.

### Gaps
- No published musculoskeletal or RL simulation of quiet standing in an ostrich, emu or other large ground bird was found.
- No simulation was found that includes the intertarsal EDM or the flamingo-style stay apparatus as explicit passive elements in a full-body model. The emu and ostrich physical joint model (Fuji Press) is a single-joint replica.
- The OstrichRL GitHub code (vittorione94/ostrichrl) was not inspected for exact foot contact geometry or leg joint stiffness and damping.

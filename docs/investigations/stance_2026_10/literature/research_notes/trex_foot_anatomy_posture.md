# Tyrannosaurus rex and large-theropod foot, ankle, centre of mass and standing posture

Scope note: the notes are aimed at choices for a MuJoCo T. rex (foot geometry, joint ranges, passive properties, target posture). "Cited Findings" hold only sourced material. "Inferences" mark my own reasoning, including what it implies for the model. Several primary texts could not be opened, mainly Brochu 2003, Hutchinson et al. 2005, Gatesy et al. 2009, Persons & Currie 2011, Farlow et al. 2000 and Lockley & Hunt 1994. Those papers are cited from abstracts or from secondary quotes, and this is flagged where it applies.

## 1. Foot structure: digitigrade stance, arctometatarsus, weight-bearing digits, hallux, phalanges, unguals, ground contact

### Takeaway
The T. rex foot was digitigrade. Three thick, load-bearing digits (II–IV, with phalangeal/pad formula 2-3-4) carried the weight, on a long, rigid, ligament-bound arctometatarsus (MT III about 650–680 mm, about 0.52–0.60 of femur length) that was held off the ground. Normally the only "heel" contact was a fleshy pad under the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint of digit IV. The small hallux (digit I) sat high on the medial side and touched the ground only in deep or occasional prints. The evidence puts the load on the proximal digits rather than at the claw tips.

### Cited Findings
- **Digitigrady.** "The dinosaurian pes is primitively digitigrade, with the metatarsophalangeal joints typically raised above the ground." The metatarsus is "an element that is not expected to have touched the ground during normal progression." — [Lallensack, Farlow & Falkingham 2022, Palaeontology (author PDF)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf)
- **"Heel" of a theropod track.** "The metatarsophalangeal pads of digits II and III seem to have been typically held clear of the ground by non-avian theropods, but may occasionally leave an impression… The metatarsophalangeal pad of digit IV, on the other hand, is thought to have commonly been impressed (Farlow et al. 2000)." — [Lallensack et al. 2022](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf)
- **"Plantigrade" theropod tracks reinterpreted.** Elongate tracks with long metatarsal marks are reinterpreted as deep penetration of a digitigrade foot into soft sediment, not flat-footed walking. Such trackways do not show reduced speeds. This contests the older, widely held "facultative plantigrade" interpretation. — [Lallensack et al. 2022](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf)
- **Arctometatarsus definition.** It is "an elongate and relatively narrow, anteroposteriorly strong metapodium with a proximal splint and distal expansion of the third metatarsal". It features "a wedge-and-buttress constriction of the central third metatarsal (MT III) between the outer bones" and rugose areas where the metatarsals articulate distally. — [Surring et al. 2022, Vertebrate Anatomy Morphology Palaeontology 10:49–64, doi:10.18435/vamp29387](https://doi.org/10.18435/vamp29387)
- **Metatarsus and limb lengths for T. rex** (femur / tibia / MT III, mm). MOR 555: 1280 / 1150 / 670. CM 9380 (cast of AMNH 973): 1269 / 1166 / 680. RTMP 81.12.1: 1200 / 1095 / 650. BHI 6230: 1100 / 1025 / 660. Juvenile LACM 23845: 825 / 825 / 508. — [Persons & Currie 2016, Sci. Rep. 6:19828, Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep19828)
- **Adult proportions.** Adult femur about 1.3 m, limb length 3–3.3 m, body length 11–12 m, tail about 60% of body length in most specimens. Limb length as a fraction of body length shortened from 33% to 26% through ontogeny. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011, PLoS ONE 6:e26037](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Phalangeal and pad formula of the main digits.** Well-preserved theropod tracks show "two phalangeal pad impressions" on digit II and four on digit IV, giving "the 2-3-4 phalangeal formula corresponding to digits II-III-IV" (pads per digit, excluding the claw). — [Lockley, Gierliński & Lucas 2011 (PDF)](https://juraparkbaltow.pl/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Lockley-Gierlinski-and-Lucas-2011.pdf); also [KU Ichnology: Minisauripus](https://ichnology.ku.edu/vertebrate_traces/dinosaurtracks/minisauripus.html)
- **Pad counts vary.** Pad counts do not always map one-to-one onto phalanges (Carmelopodus shows 2-3-3, for example). — [KU Ichnology: Carmelopodus](https://ichnology.ku.edu/vertebrate_traces/dinosaurtracks/carmelopodus.html)
- **Hallux evolution in theropods (abstract-level).** Metatarsal I was reduced, "lost direct contact with the ankle joint, and shifted more plantarly in basal neotheropods". In coelophysoids the proximal end of MT I attaches to the medioplantar margin of MT II. — [Botelho et al. 2016, JVP 36(4), doi:10.1080/02724634.2016.1116995](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724634.2016.1116995) (seen only via search snippet)
- **Tyrannosaurid track shape.** Tyrannosaur tracks are "tridactyl and, in limited cases, tetradactyl, caused by the hallux contacting the substrate". "Digits II and III are positioned in closer association to each other, whereas digit IV is typically displaced laterally." — [Boeye et al. 2026, R. Soc. Open Sci. 13:252139](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.252139)
- **Hallux in specific tracks.** In Tyrannosauripus pillmorei (the only track assigned to Tyrannosaurus) the hallux impression is medially directed. In Bellatoripes fredlundi a clawed hallux impression appears on only one print, directed cranially or craniomedially. — [McCrea et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9:e103613](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613); [KU Ichnology: Tyrannosauripus](https://ichnology.ku.edu/vertebrate_traces/dinosaurtracks/tyrannosauripus.html) (the hallux impression is "slender")
- **Digit thickness, from tyrannosaurid tracks.** TMP 81.34.1 (56.2 cm long): digit widths 16.2 cm (II), 17.2 cm (III) and 16.0 cm (IV), about 29–31% of footprint length each. Bellatoripes digit impressions are "thick proximally and taper strongly distally", with claw impressions present. — [McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)
- **Claw use in a living analogue.** In ostriches, the sharp claw tip acts as a traction element at push-off when running. In walking the claw takes "minor… participation only at push-off". — [Schaller et al. 2011, J. Exp. Biol. 214:1123, doi:10.1242/jeb.043596](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)
- **Specimen basis.** The standard osteology of the most complete specimen (FMNH PR 2081, "Sue") is Brochu 2003, SVP Memoir 7 (JVP 22 suppl.). — [doi:10.1080/02724634.2003.10010947](https://doi.org/10.1080/02724634.2003.10010947) (full text not accessed; see Gaps)

### Inferences
- **Metatarsus.** MT III/femur ratios from Persons & Currie (my arithmetic) are 0.52 (MOR 555), 0.54 (CM 9380, RTMP 81.12.1) and 0.60 (BHI 6230), with 0.62 in the juvenile. A model metatarsus about 0.5–0.6 × femur length, held clear of the ground, is anatomically right.
- **Foot contact patch.** A box pad spanning heel to toe misrepresents the real contact. Real contact is three thick digits, each with 2/3/4 pads, plus one rear pad under the digit IV MTP joint that sits laterally and caudally. The distal metatarsus/MTP region (digits II and III) is normally clear of the ground.
- **Track-based foot length.** Tracks run from the digit IV MTP pad to the digit III claw tip, so foot length measured from tracks is 0.5–0.86 m in large tyrannosaurids (see Section 4).
- **Hallux in the model.** The hallux should be non-load-bearing in quiet standing. At most it is a small, high, medially placed digit that can touch only in deep substrate.
- **Claws in the model.** Claws are traction and push-off elements, not support points. Load-bearing digit contacts in the r10 draft that extend weight support to the claw tips go against the anatomy. That design would also allow the forward CoP the project saw at 0.89–0.94 of foot length.

### Gaps
- I could not open Brochu 2003, so these values are missing: exact pedal phalanx lengths for FMNH PR 2081, digit lengths (II–IV), ungual dimensions and curvature, MT I/hallux size and the level at which it attaches on MT II. The "Sue" femur, tibia and MT lengths also come from the monograph and were not verified here. A follow-up should pull the pes measurement table from Brochu 2003 or from Farlow et al. 2013 ("Feet of the fierce", in Tyrannosaurid Paleobiology, Indiana UP), which I could not access.
- No source I reached gives a numeric ungual shape for T. rex. The common description is broad, flattened, hoof-like unguals that are only mildly curved, but I did not verify it.
- I did not verify a published value for digit IV MTP pad position or size relative to the metatarsus in tyrannosaurids.

## 2. Joint ranges and passive tissues: ankle (mesotarsal) and MTP ranges, tendons, pads, ligaments, locking or stay mechanisms

### Takeaway
No published, numeric ankle or MTP range of motion exists for T. rex. Models such as Hutchinson et al. 2005 and Sellers et al. 2017 estimate ranges from the bones and treat the ankle as a hinge, but do not publish the limits. The best quantitative proxies come from living birds: guineafowl XROMM range-of-motion envelopes, and an ostrich passive intertarsal "engage-disengage" mechanism with a 115° transition point. The strongest T. rex-specific passive-tissue evidence is the distal intermetatarsal ligaments that bound the arctometatarsus into a stiff unit. A passive tail-support ligament system has also been modelled.

### Cited Findings
- **Arctometatarsus ligaments.** Histology of a Gorgosaurus MT III shows Sharpey's fibres and pitted ligament entheses at expansive distal regions, located "for resisting torsional loading on the foot". Coelophysis lacks them, which makes it a negative control. The authors conclude tyrannosaurids "possessed distinctive and specific ligament attachments to the third metatarsal lacking in other large carnivorous dinosaurs". In adult T. rex the scars extend further proximally. These distal attachments are absent in allosauroids of similar size. The ligaments are not present in the extant bracket (birds and crocodylians), so the inference is "high-order". — [Surring et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.18435/vamp29387)
- **"Tensile keystone model".** "The triangular central metatarsal and elastic ligaments dynamically strengthened the foot… rebounding ligaments drew the outer elements towards the middle digit early in the stance phase, unifying the arctometatarsus under high loadings." This "implies, but does not demonstrate, greater agility". — [Snively & Russell 2003, J. Morphol. 255:215, doi:10.1002/jmor.10059](https://doi.org/10.1002/jmor.10059) (abstract)
- **Splay resistance.** The ligaments are described as transferring foot compression to tension "with a tensegrity-style mechanism" and minimising "intermetatarsal splay". — [Surring et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.18435/vamp29387)
- **Musculoskeletal model, Hutchinson et al. 2005.** The 3D T. rex hindlimb model had 10 degrees of freedom and 33 muscle groups crossing the hip, knee, ankle and toe joints. Moment arms were evaluated across static limb orientations. "Moment arms generally grew with joint extension, though not universally." — [Hutchinson, Anderson, Blemker & Delp 2005, Paleobiology 31:676, doi:10.1666/0094-8373(2005)031[0676:AOHMMA]2.0.CO;2](https://doi.org/10.1666/0094-8373(2005)031[0676:AOHMMA]2.0.CO;2) (abstract); [OpenSim model page](https://opensimconfluence.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/OpenSim/pages/53087785/Tyrannosaurus+Rex+Model)
- **Multibody model, Sellers et al. 2017.** All joints were hinges with flexion/extension only, sagittal plane. Joint positions and ranges of motion were "estimated directly from the skeleton" (no numbers published). Ground contact used "contact spheres on the digits" acting as stiff, damped springs. Peak bone stress at high speed fell in the metatarsal ("mid-tarsus") region and was bending-dominated. Metatarsal cortical thickness was set at 60% of mean external radius. — [Sellers et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e3420](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3420) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5518979/))
- **MTP actuator in Daspletosaurus.** A tyrannosaurid model (Daspletosaurus) needed an MTP "reserve actuator" scaled to 1,767,308 N·m, at least 27 × body weight × leg length. Without it, recruitment of the digital flexors (FDL, FDB) was excessively high. This suggests the toe joint is poorly controlled by reconstructed muscles alone. — [Bishop et al. 2018, PeerJ 6:e5777](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777) ([PMC6215443](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6215443/))
- **Hip abduction in tyrannosaurid models.** Earlier models assumed about 10° of hip abduction because the femoral head is inclined. Bringing the foot near the midline in that pose then needed about 27° of external long-axis rotation. — [Bishop et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777)
- **Guineafowl ROM (extant-bird proxy, cadaveric XROMM).** Flexion/extension: hip 95–116°, knee and intertarsal (ankle) joints each 162–176°. Abduction/adduction: hip 35–45°, decreasing distally. Long-axis rotation: hip 57–67°, knee 78–88°, ankle 36–46°. "Large portions of extension at the hip go unused during walking, running, turning." The study did not measure toe or MTP joints. — [Kambic, Roberts & Gatesy 2017, J. Anat. 231:906, doi:10.1111/joa.12680](https://doi.org/10.1111/joa.12680)
- **Toe flexion below horizontal (guineafowl).** In granular substrate the longest toe reached "an angle of 30° below horizontal before slipping backward on withdrawal". — [Falkingham & Gatesy 2014, PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.1416252111](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1416252111)
- **Looping toe path.** Toes follow a consistent "looping" path below ground, and Early Jurassic theropod tracks show the same pattern. — [Turner, Falkingham & Gatesy 2020, Biol. Lett., doi:10.1098/rsbl.2020.0309](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2020.0309)
- **Ostrich intertarsal stay mechanism.** Manual manipulation found a passive "engage-disengage mechanism (EDM)" built from ligaments interacting with the joint contours. It "establishes joint extension, provides bi-directional resistance prior to a transition point located at 115 degrees and contributes to rapid intertarsal flexion at toe off and full extension prior to touch down". Joint moments were measured in loaded specimens. — [Schaller et al. 2009, J. Anat., doi:10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x)
- **EDM in non-avian dinosaurs (unverified).** A 2025 TU Darmstadt study reportedly tested whether a palaeognath-like intertarsal EDM could have existed in a non-avian theropod, using a physical model of Avimimus. I saw only a search summary; the repository page was bot-blocked and the conclusions are unverified. — [TU Darmstadt repository record](https://tuprints.ulb.tu-darmstadt.de/30994/)
- **Ostrich plantar tissue.** The ostrich footpad's energy-damping behaviour is attributed to deformation of "fat cells and collagen fibers" (an adipose cushion). — [Zhang et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e3613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/)
- **Flamingo passive stay apparatus.** Flamingo cadavers "could passively support body weight on one leg without any muscle activity", in a stable joint posture resembling that of live birds. The cadaver "could not be stably held in a two-legged pose". In quiescent one-legged standing, "the point of force application [was] directly under the distal joint, reducing the need for muscular joint torque". — [Chang & Ting 2017, Biol. Lett. 13:20160948](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948)
- **Passive tail suspension.** The T. rex tail "was suspended passively by the caudal interspinous ligaments". The model assumed ligaments pre-strained in a horizontal "passive horizontal equilibrium" posture, with rotational springs whose torque rises quadratically with angle. Tail natural frequency was 0.66 Hz (range 0.41–0.84), giving a preferred walking speed of 1.28 m/s (0.80–1.64). Results are most sensitive to ligament moment arms and composition. — [van Bijlert, van Soest & Schulp 2021, R. Soc. Open Sci. 8:201441](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.201441)

### Inferences
- **Metatarsus as one segment.** The arctometatarsus can be modelled as one rigid segment (MT II–IV fused in effect). The ligament evidence is about resisting splay and torsion, not about adding a degree of freedom.
- **Ankle as a hinge.** One flexion/extension DOF at the ankle (mesotarsal) is the norm in published T. rex models (Sellers 2017; Hutchinson 2005 used a small number of DOFs). Bird ROM data (about 160–176° of total intertarsal flexion/extension in guineafowl) are an upper bound; standing would use only a small part of it.
- **Ankle stay mechanism.** A passive ankle-extension stay (in the spirit of the ostrich EDM, with a transition at about 115° included angle) is a defensible "plausible but unproven for non-avian theropods" option. It should be flagged as speculative if added.
- **MTP joint.** The toe joint is weakly specified by the anatomy. Published models either add reserve torque (Bishop 2018) or use simple contacts on the digits (Sellers 2017). A stiff, damped passive MTP with limited hyperextension is consistent with these choices. No source gives a T. rex-specific number.
- **Toe placement under the joint.** The flamingo result is the clearest extant evidence for cheap standing. The ground-force point sits directly under the distal joint, so joint torque is close to zero. The model's analogue would put the CoP near the MTP/proximal-digit region, not near the toe tips.

### Gaps
- I found no published numeric ROM for T. rex ankle or MTP joints, and none for any large non-avian theropod. Hutchinson et al. 2005 and Gatesy et al. 2009 may contain pose ranges explored, but their full texts were not accessible.
- I found no direct fossil evidence of digital flexor tendons, plantar fat pads or a digital tendon-locking mechanism in tyrannosaurids. Pad existence is inferred only from track pad impressions (Section 4).
- I found no T. rex-specific passive locking mechanism at the knee, ankle or MTP in peer-reviewed literature.

## 3. Centre of mass and posture: COM estimates, limb posture, moment arms, ability to stand

### Takeaway
Every published whole-body reconstruction puts the T. rex COM in front of and below the hip. Best estimates are about 0.45–0.6 m cranial, with a full range of about 0.3–0.8 m. That is roughly 30–45% of femur length (Sue 44–61%), or about 3–7% of body length cranial to the hip, and about 0.3–0.5 m below it. Posture analyses agree that adult T. rex stood and moved with a fairly upright limb: subvertical femur and slightly flexed joints, but not fully columnar and not bird-like crouched. Its hip extensors had to resist the forward COM.

### Cited Findings
- **Hutchinson et al. 2011, five specimens.** Minimal-to-maximal mass ranges: Stan and MOR 555 about 5800–10800 kg; Carnegie about 7400–14600 kg; Sue about 9500–18500 kg (minimal Sue 9502 kg); juvenile Jane 639–1269 kg. Maximal models were about 1.9 × minimal. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011, PLoS ONE 6:e26037](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **COM in Hutchinson et al. 2011.** Minimal models put the COM about 0.55 m cranial to the hip for Carnegie, Stan and MOR 555, and 0.80 m for Sue. It sat about 0.34 m below the hip (0.495 m for Stan). Normalised to femur length, the "most plausible" adult cranial COM range is about 30–45% (Sue 44–61%). Extreme models span −1.9% (called "highly implausible") to 88.8%. Dorsoventral COM is 22–35% of femur length below the hip in Carnegie and MOR, 26–28% in Sue and 38–50% in Stan. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Hip extensors and COM.** Hutchinson et al. 2011 suggest the hip muscles needed to "balance the cranially-positioned COM", which may explain the large hip extensors. A craniodorsal COM shift could require "greater active muscle volumes around the hip to stabilize the COM". — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Segment masses (% body mass, minimal models).** Head 4.1–11.1, neck 3.2–7.3, torso about 54–59 (MOR 35.3), tail 10–21, thigh 12.6–17.4, shank 4.3–5.9, foot 1.3–2.9, forelimb 0.1–0.4. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Muscle masses (Hutchinson et al. 2011).** Caudofemoralis longus 162–189 kg (1.95–3.9% body mass). Hip extensors 8.8–15.1%, knee extensors 3.3–7.1%, ankle extensors 1.9–3.2%, total hindlimb extensors about 14–25% of body mass. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Bates et al. 2009, BHI 3033 (best estimate 7655 kg).** Whole-body COM 0.572 m cranial and 0.297 m below the hip, 4.9% of body length. Sensitivity range 0.385–0.784 m cranial (3.3–6.71% of body length). — [Bates et al. 2009, PLoS ONE 4:e4532, Table 8](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004532)
- **Bates et al. 2009, MOR 555.** Best-estimate COM 0.468 m cranial and 0.326 m below the hip (4.22% of body length). Range 0.295–0.652 m (2.66–5.89%). For comparison, the Hutchinson et al. (2007) best-estimate MOR 555 COM was 0.51 m cranial of the hip. — [Bates et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004532)
- **COM always ahead of the hip.** "Our sensitivity analysis consistently places the centre of mass well below and in front of hip joint in each animal… Even in models with significantly enlarged tails and reduced thoracic and neck segments the CM still remained comfortably in front of the hip joint". Across the five taxa, enlarged-tail models gave 0.78–6.2% of body length anterior. — [Bates et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004532)
- **Hutchinson, Ng-Thow-Hing & Anderson 2007.** B-spline body segment method applied to adult T. rex, with 30 combinations of trunk, lung, air-sac and limb dimensions. — [J. Theor. Biol. 246:660, doi:10.1016/j.jtbi.2007.01.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2007.01.023)
- **Effect of a broader tail (Snively et al. 2019).** Giving the tail a 1.4 × cross-section shifted modelled COMs "posteriorly by 5–15%… placing them closer to the anteroposterior location of the acetabulum". In large-tail models of Acrocanthosaurus and Sinraptor the COM was coincident with the acetabulum. FMNH PR 2081 masses were 8302–8692 kg (slender), 9131 kg (broad) and 9713 kg (40% broader tail). — [Snively et al. 2019, PeerJ 7:e6432](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6432) ([PMC6387760](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6387760/))
- **Rotational inertia.** Large theropods minimised the increase in rotational inertia about a vertical axis as size increased. — [Henderson & Snively 2004, Proc. R. Soc. B 271:S57, doi:10.1098/rsbl.2003.0097](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2003.0097)
- **Gradual crouch along the bird line.** Results support "a gradual, stepwise acquisition of more-crouched limb postures across much of theropod evolution", accelerating in Maniraptora. Tail reduction was not the main correlate of the COM shift; forelimb enlargement mattered more. — [Allen, Bates, Li & Hutchinson 2013, Nature 497:104](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12059)
- **Challenge to the dichotomy.** Macaulay et al. 2023 challenge the simple "non-avian theropod COM near hip versus bird COM cranial" split. They find terrestrial and volant taxa occupy distinct COM morphospace, and that tail mass correlates strongly with craniocaudal COM (Rho = −0.989). — [Macaulay et al. 2023, Nat. Commun., doi:10.1038/s41467-023-37317-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37317-y)
- **Running ability (Hutchinson & Garcia 2002).** To run quickly, adult T. rex "would have needed an unreasonably large mass of extensor muscle, even with generous assumptions". Animals of about 6000 kg were "doubtful" runners. Press coverage reported about 86% of body mass in leg muscle for about 20 m/s; a later summary says 43% per hindlimb. — [Hutchinson & Garcia 2002, Nature 415:1018](https://doi.org/10.1038/4151018a); [Physics Today summary](https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/how-fast-could-tyrannosaurus-rex-run)
- **Upright limbs (Hutchinson 2004).** Results "strengthen the contention that many nonavian theropods, especially larger individuals, used fairly upright limb orientations". "Ankle extensor muscle support is shown to have been important for all taxa", and hip extensor capacity "appears to be a crucial limit" in large taxa. — [Hutchinson 2004, J. Morphol. 262:441, doi:10.1002/jmor.10240](https://doi.org/10.1002/jmor.10240)
- **Moment arms (Hutchinson et al. 2005).** "More upright poses would have been the most effective for generating the muscle moments required to stand and move, but completely columnar poses were not as effective as those with at least slight limb flexion." T. rex lacked the large moment arms expected of a proficient runner. — [Hutchinson et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1666/0094-8373(2005)031[0676:AOHMMA]2.0.CO;2) (abstract); [OpenSim page](https://opensimconfluence.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/OpenSim/pages/53087785/Tyrannosaurus+Rex+Model)
- **Pose exclusion (Gatesy et al. 2009).** Excluding poses by joint and force constraints left T. rex with "a narrow band of poses that are neither very upright nor strongly flexed". These permit slow running at most. — [Gatesy, Bäker & Hutchinson 2009, JVP 29:535](https://doi.org/10.1671/039.029.0213) (abstract via search)
- **Initial posture rule in Bishop et al. 2018.** The hip angle was set "so that the knee joint was near the line of the vertical ground reaction force" in the sagittal plane, following Gatesy et al. 2009. — [Bishop et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777)
- **Cancellous-bone posture for Daspletosaurus.** Bone architecture indicates a "largely upright" posture "with a subvertical femoral orientation" at about mid-stance, more upright than Troodon and much more than birds. COM anterior position and degree of crouch change gradually towards birds. — [Bishop et al. 2018, PeerJ 6:e5777](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.5777)
- **Speed limit (Sellers et al. 2017).** BHI 3033 modelled at 7206.7 kg with muscle at 50% of body mass. Speed was limited by bone stress to about 7.7 m/s only at stress limits above 200 MPa; at a 100 MPa safety limit the Froude number was about 1. True running "would probably lead to unacceptably high skeletal loads". — [Sellers et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3420)
- **Hip-height range.** For MOR 555, standing hip height to the proximal pedal phalanx is 2.5 m at a "75% crouch" and 3.15 m at "95% crouch". Most columnar poses were judged of concern. — [Boeye et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.252139)
- **Caudofemoralis and the tail (Persons & Currie 2011).** The M. caudofemoralis of many non-avian theropods was "exceptionally large". In Tyrannosaurus it was further enlarged by deep haemal arches. — [Persons & Currie 2011, Anat. Rec. 294:119](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.21290) (abstract)
- **Contested CFL mass.** Persons & Currie's estimate for Stan (261 kg) was criticised by Hutchinson et al. 2011 as inflating relative mass through a low body-mass assumption. Press coverage reported that the larger tail moves the COM slightly backward and that earlier tail-muscle estimates were up to 45% too low. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/animals/t-rex-tail-muscles-speed-101118.html)

### Inferences
- **Converting the COM to model units.** With a femur of about 1.3 m and hip height about 2.5–3.1 m, the consensus COM (about 0.45–0.6 m cranial, 0.3–0.35 m below the hip) is about 0.15–0.2 × hip height ahead of the hip and about 0.1 × hip height below it. A heavier tail moves it towards the hip (Snively 2019). The project's T. rex model COM, measured in its standing pose, should fall in this band. If it sits much further forward, the mass distribution, not the reward, is driving the lean.
- **Standing pose.** Static balance needs the COM vertically over the foot contact. With the COM 0.4–0.6 m ahead of the hip, the anatomically supported pose has a near-vertical femur (Daspletosaurus "subvertical"), slight knee and ankle flexion and a steep metatarsus. The digits then sit under the COM, with the knee near the vertical ground-reaction line (Gatesy 2009 / Bishop 2018 rule). Hip extensor torque, mainly the caudofemoralis and hamstring-like muscles, resists the forward moment.
- **Target posture.** "Slightly flexed, not columnar" is the consensus target. Fully straight legs are less effective in the Hutchinson 2005 moment-arm analysis. Crouched, bird-like poses need far more extensor muscle (Hutchinson & Garcia 2002; Hutchinson 2004).
- **What the forward lean may reflect.** A learned forward lean onto the toes is not supported by any posture study. All of them place the support under the COM with the limb near-vertical. A reward term rewarding CoP near the MTP/proximal-digit region, or the COM projection over the rear-middle of the contact patch, would be consistent with the anatomy.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full texts of Hutchinson et al. 2005, Gatesy et al. 2009 or Hutchinson 2004, which hold the specific joint angles (hip, knee, ankle, MTP) of plausible mid-stance poses. Bishop 2018's Daspletosaurus joint angles are only in figures. Those figures are the place to get numeric target angles.
- Allen et al. 2013 (paywalled) may list a T. rex COM value; not retrieved.
- No study I found estimates the COM relative to the foot (as a fraction of foot length) for a standing T. rex.

## 4. Footprint and trackway evidence: tyrannosaurid tracks, pads, depth, resting traces, pubic boot

### Takeaway
Large tyrannosaurid tracks are 50–86 cm long, functionally tridactyl and narrow-gauge. A rear "metatarsal/MTP pad" impression is common; a hallux impression is rare. Where depth varies, the deepest areas are around the proximal phalanges, especially digit III, then IV, rather than the claws. The only well-documented sitting trace is an Early Jurassic one, not tyrannosaurid. It shows weight on the metatarsi and an ischial pad with the legs folded symmetrically. The idea that T. rex rested on its pubic boot is plausible but comes from non-peer-reviewed or old sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Tyrannosauripus pillmorei** (Philmont Scout Ranch, New Mexico; Lockley & Hunt 1994, Ichnos 3:213–218). About 86 cm long × 64 cm wide. A slightly asymmetrical tetradactyl track, with width about two-thirds of length and a slender hallux impression. Pillmore's own account ("almost 3 feet long and 2 feet wide") conflicts with this. — [Wikipedia: Tyrannosauripus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrannosauripus); [KU Ichnology](https://ichnology.ku.edu/vertebrate_traces/dinosaurtracks/tyrannosauripus.html)
- **Tyrannosauripus versus Bellatoripes.** Tyrannosauripus prints are "about 40% longer and 20% wider" than Bellatoripes, with "indistinct digital pads and a medially directed hallux". — [McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)
- **Bellatoripes fredlundi (Wapiti Formation, British Columbia).** Holotype footprint length 62.0 cm, area 1509 cm², diagnosis FL > 50 cm. Functionally tridactyl, mesaxonic, digitigrade. Digits II and IV of similar length; digit III has a short free length relative to footprint length. Total divarication "generally no greater than 80°", with III–IV > II–III. No digital pad impressions. The metatarsal pad impression has a wide caudal margin, sometimes with 2–3 lobes. Skin tubercle impressions are present. — [McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)
- **Bellatoripes trackway.** Pace 155–175 cm, stride about 350 cm, pace angulation near 180°, narrow trackway with prints overlapping the midline. Estimated hip height 2.30 m (flexed), 2.80 m (general equation) or 2.87 m (straight leg). Speed 6.4–8.5 km/h. Smaller referred tracks of 49–52 cm show visible digital pads and are suggested to be juveniles. — [McCrea et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0103613)
- **Wapiti tyrannosaurid tracks (Enriquez et al. 2021, JVP).** Tracks range from about 40 cm (juvenile) to more than 60 cm. Geometric morphometrics suggest juveniles had more slender feet than adults. — [UNE press release](https://www.une.edu.au/about-une/news-and-events/news/2021/04/fat-footed-tyrannosaur-parents-could-not-keep-up-with-their-skinnier-adolescent-offspring); [Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/paleontology/tyrannosaur-feet-09585.html) (secondary sources; paper not accessed)
- **Depth pattern in tyrannosaur tracks.** "In some well-preserved trackways of tyrannosaurs, digits III and IV appear to have a greater depth than impressions left by digit II, creating two distinct depressions in areas around the proximal pedal phalanges, with one centred on digits II and III and a second on digit IV." The foot-strike is described as "distal-first", with the flesh under the distal phalanges contacting first. — [Boeye et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.252139)
- **Ostrich toe loading (living analogue).** The 3rd toe bore about 77% (solid ground) to 84% (sand) of the load and the 4th toe 23% / 14%. The CoP moved from the lateral part to the centre of the 3rd toe and then to the claw only after about 70% of stance. — [Zhang et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e3613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5530993/)
- **Ostrich CoP pattern (Schaller et al. 2011).** The 4th toe acts as a balance outrigger: "modulation of 4th toe pressure… allows correction of balance". Walking and running share a J-shaped CoP trajectory. — [Schaller et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)
- **Theropod resting trace, St George, Utah** (Eubrontes, Early Jurassic, about 198 Ma; Dilophosaurus-sized maker). The animal sat with legs folded symmetrically, both feet parallel, and "the weight of the body was distributed between each metatarsus and pes". It left elongate, nearly symmetrical metatarsal "heel" impressions and circular ischial callosity impressions. The hands touched down craniolateral to the feet with palms facing medially. The proximal tail was held elevated and the distal tail dragged (mark 31 cm long, 134 cm behind the ischial callosity). The animal shuffled about 25 cm forward, rested again and then stood. Only six crouching or resting theropod traces with adequate data had been reported, four lacking manus impressions. — [Milner et al. 2009, PLoS ONE 4:e4591](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004591)
- **Resting trace interpretation.** Resting traces (metatarsal plus ischial impressions) must be distinguished from elongate locomotion tracks, which are deep penetration. — [Lallensack et al. 2022](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15899/1/Lallensack%20Farlow%20Falkingham%202021%20Palaeontology%20elongate%20tracks.pdf)
- **Pubic boot as a resting support (not peer-reviewed).** "It has long been expected that the great theropod would have settled its weight upon its pubic boot". A DinoMorph articulation of "Stan" shows the hindlimb can take a broad range of crouched positions while resting on it. Newman (1970) proposed the forelimbs acted as a "brake" holding the body while the hindlimbs extended to rise. This later became the "push-up" idea. — [K. Stevens, DinoMorph page](https://ix.cs.uoregon.edu/~kent/paleontology/Tyrannosaurus/RexSit.html) (non-peer-reviewed); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/if-t-rex-fell-how-did-it) (secondary; it notes the push-up idea is contested and that rising "as birds do", with the tail as counterbalance, is an alternative)

### Inferences
- **Load location.** The ichnology (proximal-phalanx depressions, MTP pad impression, claws as tapering marks) and ostrich pedobarography (about 80% on digit III, CoP on the toe's middle until late stance) agree. Load in a theropod foot centres on the proximal–middle part of the digits, especially digit III, with digit IV as a lateral stabiliser.
- **CoP estimate for quiet standing (unverified).** For quiet standing I would expect the CoP at roughly 0.3–0.55 of track length from the rear (MTP-pad) margin. The figure is unpublished, so treat it as a working hypothesis. The model's r9 CoP (0.76–0.79) and r10 CoP (0.89–0.94) both lie forward of what the tracks and the extant analogues suggest for standing. Note that the model's "foot length" may not equal track length.
- **Divarication for model digits.** Digits should spread to at most 80° in total, with II and III closer together and IV angled laterally. The narrow trackway gauge (prints overlapping the midline) means a narrow mediolateral support base when walking.
- **Resting pose.** The animal settles onto the metatarsi (tarsus and metatarsus horizontal on the ground) plus the ventral pelvis: the ischial callosity in the Jurassic trace, and plausibly the pubic boot in tyrannosaurs. It is not a toe-standing posture. This bears on any "sit/rest" or recovery task, not on standing.

### Gaps
- I could not open Manning et al. 2008 (probable Hell Creek tyrannosaurid track, Palaios), Lockley & Hunt 1994 or Enriquez et al. 2021, so pad-by-pad depth data and digit lengths from tyrannosaurid tracks are missing.
- I found no published pressure or depth map of a tyrannosaurid track giving percent load per digit.
- I found no resting or sitting trace attributable to a tyrannosaurid. The pubic-boot resting role is untested in peer-reviewed literature as far as I found.

## 5. Standing balance: heavy head and tail, tail as counterweight, static stability

### Takeaway
The long tail does not move the T. rex COM behind the hip. In every reconstruction the COM stays ahead of the hip, so standing needs continuous hip-extensor torque or a passive equivalent, with the feet under the COM. Recent work stresses the tail's dynamic and passive-elastic roles over a purely static counterweight: angular-momentum regulation, and ligament-suspended, spring-like support. Extant birds show passive "stay" mechanisms that make standing cheap when the ground force sits under the distal joint.

### Cited Findings
- **Tail and COM.** Even "models with significantly enlarged tails and reduced thoracic and neck segments" keep the COM "comfortably in front of the hip joint". — [Bates et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0004532)
- **Larger tails.** A 1.4 × tail cross-section moves the COM 5–15% posteriorly towards the acetabulum. — [Snively et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6432)
- **Tail and head mass.** The tail is 10–21% of body mass and the head plus neck about 7–18% (Section 3). Hip muscles must "balance the cranially-positioned COM". — [Hutchinson et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037)
- **Tail as a dynamic balancer (Coelophysis).** "Rather than just being a static counterbalance… the tail played a crucial dynamic role, with lateroflexion acting as a passive, physics-based mechanism for regulating angular momentum and improving locomotor economy". Making the tail passive (no active tail joint control) "had a negligible effect". Changes in tail mass had minimal effect "because of the greater influence of distance than mass on angular momentum". Removing the proximal-tail mass made the model over-recruit its MTP torque motor. — [Bishop et al. 2021, Sci. Adv. 7:eabi7348](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abi7348) ([PMC8457660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8457660/))
- **Passive tail suspension (T. rex).** The tail is held near horizontal passively by pre-strained interspinous ligaments. — [van Bijlert et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.201441)
- **Turning inertia.** Tyrannosaurids had low rotational inertia relative to body mass and could turn more quickly than other large theropods. Large theropods generally minimised rotational inertia about a vertical axis as size increased. — [Snively et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.6432); [Henderson & Snively 2004](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2003.0097)
- **Passive standing in flamingos.** One-legged standing in flamingos is passively stable in cadavers, while two-legged standing is not. In live birds, sway falls during quiescence and the force point sits directly under the distal joint. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948)
- **Ostrich intertarsal EDM.** The passive engage-disengage mechanism resists motion in both directions around a 115° transition and helps hold the joint extended. — [Schaller et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x)
- **Ostrich balance via digit IV.** Balance is corrected by modulating 4th-toe pressure, mostly early in stance and at low speed. — [Schaller et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)
- **Hip extensor capacity.** For large theropods, "the ability of hip extensor muscles to support the body appears to be a crucial limit". — [Hutchinson 2004](https://doi.org/10.1002/jmor.10240)
- **Standing upright.** More upright, slightly flexed poses are the most effective for "generat[ing] the muscle moments required to stand". — [Hutchinson et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1666/0094-8373(2005)031[0676:AOHMMA]2.0.CO;2)
- **Rising from rest.** Proposals range from forelimbs used as a brake or push (Newman 1970, via secondary source) to rising "as birds do" with the tail as counterbalance. Neither has been tested biomechanically in a peer-reviewed study I found. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/if-t-rex-fell-how-did-it); [Stevens DinoMorph page](https://ix.cs.uoregon.edu/~kent/paleontology/Tyrannosaurus/RexSit.html)

### Inferences
- **Hip torque in standing.** Since the COM is ahead of the hip, a policy that stands with the GRF under the COM must hold a net hip-extension torque. Leaning forward onto the toes moves the GRF line further ahead of the hip and knee and raises the extensor demand. Anatomically this is the costly, not the natural, solution, which supports adding a fore-aft CoP or COM-placement term.
- **Passive stiffness in the model.** Passive elements are biologically defensible if flagged as hypotheses. Candidates are a pre-strained, spring-suspended tail (van Bijlert), a stiff arctometatarsus (Surring/Snively) and possibly an ankle stay or EDM (ostrich, unproven in non-avian theropods). They can lower the active torque needed to hold a neutral stance.
- **Tail mass and inertia.** Tail mass changes have little dynamic effect compared with tail length or distance (Bishop 2021). For balance, the tail's length and inertia matter more than small changes to its mass.

### Gaps
- I found no published static-stability analysis (support polygon, CoP margins, sway) for a standing T. rex or any large non-avian theropod. All the quantitative work concerns mass properties, locomotion or speed.
- I found no peer-reviewed estimate of how much hip-extensor torque or activation T. rex needed for quiet standing.

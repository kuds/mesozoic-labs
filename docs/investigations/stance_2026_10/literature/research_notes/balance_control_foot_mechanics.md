# Standing-balance mechanics and control in bipeds, with emphasis on the foot (CoP, foot geometry, ankle mechanics, foot sensing)

Scope note: human physiology sources were checked against their PubMed abstracts (DOIs from PubMed). The DOIs of the robotics sources were checked against Crossref. For most IEEE papers the full abstract could not be retrieved (Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex rate-limited this session). Robotics claims are therefore kept to what the title or a retrieved abstract or bibliography entry supports, and the source of each claim is marked. ## 1. Centre of pressure and support: support polygon, CoP/ZMP limits, margin of stability, extrapolated CoM / capture point, and how foot length and toe joints change the usable CoP range

### Takeaway
The CoP (equal to the ZMP on flat ground) can only lie inside the support polygon. Static stability needs the CoM projection inside it. Dynamic stability needs the *extrapolated* CoM (XcoM = x + v/ω0, which equals the instantaneous capture point) inside it. The margin of stability is the distance from the XcoM to the nearest edge, so a biped that parks its CoP or CoM near the toe edge has almost no forward margin, whatever the foot length. Longer feet or toes move the edge forward, but the margin is measured from the operating point to the edge. Moving the operating point forward with the edge gives no extra robustness.

### Cited Findings
- **XcoM / margin of stability (Hof et al. 2005).** Static condition: the vertical projection of the CoM must lie within the base of support (BoS). Dynamic extension from the inverted-pendulum model: "the position of (the vertical projection of) the CoM plus its velocity times a factor (square root l/g) should be within the BoS", where l is leg length. This is named the extrapolated centre of mass (XcoM). The margin of stability *b* is "the minimum distance from XcoM to the boundaries of the BoS". An alternative is the temporal margin τ, "the time in which the boundary of the BoS would be reached without intervention". The paper reports data for standing on one or two feet, flat-footed and on tiptoe. — [Hof, Gazendam & Sinke 2005, J Biomech 38:1-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.03.025)
- **Velocity-to-CoP conversion rule (Hof 2008).** XcoM ξ = x + v/ω0, with ω0 "a constant related to stature". "A disturbance which results in a CoM velocity change Δv can be compensated by a change in foot position (CoP) equal to Δv/ω0 in the same direction." — [Hof 2008, Hum Mov Sci 27:112-125](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.humov.2007.08.003)
- **Lateral ankle strategy in walking (Hof et al. 2010).** It is the fastest response, with a mechanical delay of about 200 ms, "but it can displace the centre of pressure no more than 2 cm". Stepping needs at least 300 ms but has a range of 20 cm. The CoP-shift (ankle) strategy is fast but range-limited by foot width or length. — [Hof, Vermerris & Gjaltema 2010, J Exp Biol 213:2655-2664](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.042572)
- **Capture point (Pratt et al. 2006).** Defined the capture point and capture region: the ground point(s) where a biped can step to come to a complete stop. For the linear inverted pendulum (LIPM) there is a single instantaneous capture point, computable from CoM position and velocity. It is the same expression as Hof's XcoM. — [Pratt, Carff, Drakunov & Goswami 2006, IEEE-RAS Humanoids](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHR.2006.321385). The flywheel detail comes from a secondary source (a patent by the same group): adding a flywheel (centroidal angular momentum) to the LIPM grows the single capture point into "a line segment capture region". — [US Patent 7,949,430 (Pratt/Goswami et al.), via Justia](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7949430)
- **Capturability with finite feet (Koolen et al. 2012).** Formalises N-step capturability for the LIPM and variants with finite-sized feet and a flywheel. A state is "0-step capturable" (recoverable without stepping) when the instantaneous capture point lies inside the region the CoP can reach, the base of support. This is from my knowledge of the paper; only the title and DOI were checked this session. — [Koolen, de Boer, Rebula, Goswami & Pratt 2012, IJRR 31:1094-1113](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364912452673)
- **ZMP and the support polygon.** The ZMP must lie in the support polygon. Reaching its edge means the foot is about to rotate about that edge (title and DOI checked; the content is the standard ZMP result). — [Vukobratović & Borovac 2004, Int J Humanoid Robotics 1:157-173](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219843604000083). The CoP and the ZMP coincide for a foot on flat ground. — [Sardain & Bessonnet 2004, IEEE TSMC-A 34:630-637](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMCA.2004.832811)
- **Foot-rotation indicator (FRI).** Goswami's FRI is the ground point where the reaction force would have to act to keep the foot from rotating. When the FRI leaves the support polygon the foot rotates (tips about the toe or heel edge), and its distance from the edge serves as a margin (from my knowledge of the paper; title and DOI checked). — [Goswami 1999, IJRR 18:523-533](https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649922066376)
- **CMP vs ZMP.** Ground reference points: the centroidal moment pivot (CMP) differs from the ZMP/CoP whenever whole-body angular momentum about the CoM changes. This is the formal link to "hip/tail" strategies, which generate moments about the CoM (from my knowledge; title and DOI checked). — [Popovic, Goswami & Herr 2005, IJRR 24:1013-1032](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364905058363)
- **Usable CoP range in humans is well inside the geometric foot.** Young subjects (eyes open) could move the mean CoP over a maximum AP range of "about 60% of foot length"; about 50% with eyes closed. Elderly: about 40% (EO) and 30% (EC). Parkinsonians: about 30%. — [Schieppati et al. 1994, Electroencephalogr Clin Neurophysiol 93:286-298](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0)
- **Functional base of support (FBOS).** FBOS (forward-to-backward sustained-lean CoP difference ÷ foot length) was 0.60 ± 0.07 under age 60 and 0.42 ± 0.12 at 60+, declining about 16% per decade after 60. Forward lean and backward lean made up 66% and 34% of FBOS respectively. — [King, Judge & Wolfson 1994, J Gerontol 49:M258-263](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronj/49.6.m258)
- **Humans can hold CoP targets from 20 to 80% of foot length from the heel.** Reference positions spanned 20–80 %FL in 5 %FL steps. Extreme positions (20–30 %FL and 70–80 %FL) were reproduced more precisely than positions near quiet stance (40–60 %FL). — [Fujiwara et al. 2010, J Physiol Anthropol 29:197-203](https://doi.org/10.2114/jpa2.29.197)
- **Toes and the forward limit (humans).** Forward leaning increased soleus EMG and "induced activity in FDB" (flexor digitorum brevis, a toe flexor). FDB was more cross-correlated with CoP in forward inclination than in normal stance, which suggests a role "in the fine-tuning of postural adjustment during forward leaning". — [Schieppati et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0). Constraining the great toe worsened single-leg stance and "directional control ability during forward/backward weight shifting", but made no difference in two-footed quiet stance. — [Chou et al. 2009, J Orthop Res 27:549-554](https://doi.org/10.1002/jor.20661). In older adults, fallers had weaker hallux (11.6 vs 14.8 %BW) and lesser-toe flexors (8.7 vs 10.8 %BW). — [Mickle et al. 2009, Clin Biomech 24:787-791](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clinbiomech.2009.08.011). Toe pressure weakness and reduced great-toe tactile sense were associated with postural instability. — [Tanaka et al. 1996, IEEE Trans Rehabil Eng 4:84-90](https://doi.org/10.1109/86.506405)
- **Toe joints on humanoids (walking-focused).** Passive toe joints on HRP-2: the abstract (via the AIST bibliography entry, partly truncated) attributes longer steps to "an under-actuated phase" with "an appropriate ZMP trajectory" and reports smoother and roughly "1.5" times faster walking in simulation. — [Sellaouti, Stasse, Kajita, Yokoi & Kheddar 2006, IROS](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2006.282449); [AIST bib entry](https://unit.aist.go.jp/jrl-22022/assets/bib/sellaouti_iros_2006.bib). Other toe work: toe springs for ZMP-based running on HRP-2LR — [Kajita et al. 2007, ICRA](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.2007.364087); active toe joints for walking and full-body motion — [Nishiwaki et al. 2002, ICRA](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.2002.1013704); low-cost passive toes on SURENA III — [Sadedel et al. 2016, Robotica](https://doi.org/10.1017/S026357471600059X); a standing-specific study — [Kouchaki & Sadigh 2014, "Effect of toe-joint and heel height on balancing of a standing biped", ASME IMECE](https://doi.org/10.1115/IMECE2014-38350) (abstract not retrieved).
- A secondary review statement found in search says a flexible foot "enables the ZMP to be moved continuously from heel to toe even in a single support phase". The results trace it to a toe-joint review, but its primary source was not pinned down. — [search result: Sadedel et al. / Semnan J Modeling in Engineering 2018](https://modelling.semnan.ac.ir/article_3046_en.html)

### Inferences
- **Margin math the project can use directly.** For an inverted pendulum of height l, ω0 = √(g/l). Using √(g/l) and √(l/g): l = 1 m gives ω0 ≈ 3.13 s⁻¹ and time constant ≈ 0.32 s; l = 2 m gives ω0 ≈ 2.21 s⁻¹ and ≈ 0.45 s. Per Hof 2008, the largest forward CoM-velocity disturbance absorbable without stepping is about b·ω0, where b is the distance from the XcoM to the toe edge. With the CoP and CoM resting 1 cm from the toe edge and ω0 ≈ 2–3 s⁻¹, that is only about 0.02–0.03 m/s, so tiny action-noise impulses push the XcoM out. This matches the project's failure under joint-space action noise.
- **Why longer load-bearing toes made the lean worse.** Extending the support polygon forward moves the boundary but not the policy's incentive. If nothing in the reward or observation ties the CoP to the middle of the foot, a policy that drifts forward ends up at the new edge. It "survives" longer (termination later) yet keeps a near-zero margin. The robust quantity is the *distance between operating CoP/XcoM and edge*, not foot length. Humans keep quiet-stance CoP far from both functional limits (see section 5).
- **Usable vs geometric polygon.** Even healthy young humans use only about 60% of foot length as their voluntary CoP range (Schieppati; King). The extreme toe and heel regions are reserved: tissue compliance, toe-flexor force limits and reduced sensory precision make the true limit smaller than the geometric outline. A simulated box foot with rigid edges gives full-length CoP authority, which may make toe-edge balancing look viable in simulation but not on hardware.
- **Digitigrade transfer.** In a theropod-like foot the ground contact is the digits plus a metatarsophalangeal pad. The "heel" edge of the support polygon sits near the MTP joint, far forward of the true ankle (intertarsal joint). Backward CoP authority is therefore structurally small unless the contact pad extends behind the MTP joint. A design that places the whole-body CoM projection near the middle of the digit/pad contact area matters more than for a plantigrade human, whose heel provides generous backward range. This is an inference from geometry; no paper measured it for theropods.
- **Toe joints are mostly a walking feature.** In humans the toes mainly help near the forward limit, in single-leg stance and in weight shifting (Schieppati; Chou), not in quiet two-legged stance. For a standing policy, a toe joint or long toe is not a reason to operate further forward. A compliant or passive toe also makes the CoP-to-joint-torque relationship softer near the toe edge.

### Gaps
- Hof et al. 2005's tabulated margin values for flat-foot vs tiptoe stance were not retrievable (abstract only). No numeric quiet-stance margin b is quoted here.
- Found no primary source giving a quantitative "usable CoP range vs foot length or toe-joint stiffness" rule for robots. The Kouchaki & Sadigh 2014 standing study is the closest, but its abstract and results were not retrieved.
- Sellaouti 2006's "1.5× faster" figure comes from a truncated abstract snippet. Its baseline was not verified.

## 2. Ankle versus hip strategies (Horak & Nashner 1986; Kuo & Zajac 1993): when each is used, and implications for a biped with a long heavy tail and head

### Takeaway
Humans use the ankle strategy (moving the CoP under the foot) for small or slow perturbations on full-length support. They add a hip strategy (counter-rotating segments to produce a horizontal shear force or moment about the CoM) when the support is short relative to the foot, when perturbations are fast, or when the CoP is near its limit. The hip strategy is mechanically cheaper per unit of CoM acceleration, but no CoP authority is required for it. Mechanically, a long heavy tail and head are a large counter-rotating "flywheel" for the second of Hof's three balance mechanisms. This could give a dinosaur biped a powerful non-foot balance channel, which the policy should use instead of pushing the CoP to the toe edge.

### Cited Findings
- **Horak & Nashner 1986.** On a normal support surface, AP surface translations evoked stereotyped muscle activation (73–110 ms latencies) starting at the ankle and radiating proximally. This produced ankle torques that restored equilibrium: the "ankle strategy". On a surface "short in relation to foot length", trunk and thigh muscles fired in the opposite (proximal-to-distal) sequence with ankle muscles "generally unresponsive". This produced "a compensatory horizontal shear force against the support surface but little, if any, ankle torque": the "hip strategy". Intermediate surface lengths produced mixtures. — [Horak & Nashner 1986, J Neurophysiol 55:1369-1381](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1986.55.6.1369)
- **Kuo & Zajac 1993.** With straight knees, the feasible-acceleration set "greatly favors a combination of ankle and hip movement in the ratio 1:3 (called the 'hip strategy')". The "ankle strategy … requires more muscle activation than the 'hip strategy' for a given amount of horizontal acceleration". "When near the boundary of instability … the movement strategy necessary to maintain stability, without taking a step, is quite restricted." — [Kuo & Zajac 1993, Prog Brain Res 97:349-358](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0079-6123(08)62294-3)
- **Strategies form a continuum (Runge et al. 1999).** Backward platform translations from 5 to 55 cm/s on a flat surface added a hip flexor torque to the ankle plantarflexor torque as velocity increased, "a continuum of postural responses". Pure hip torque without ankle torque was never observed. — [Runge, Shupert, Horak & Zajac 1999, Gait Posture 10:161-170](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0966-6362(99)00032-6)
- **Optimal control explanation.** An LQ/optimal-estimation model with time delay, weighting CoM excursion and upright deviation plus effort, reproduced the observed strategy selection. — [Kuo 1995, IEEE Trans Biomed Eng 42:87-101](https://doi.org/10.1109/10.362914)
- **Quiet stance is ankle-dominated.** "Ankle mechanisms dominate during normal stance especially in the sagittal plane". Narrow stance width "decreased the role of the ankle and increased the role of hip mechanisms". — [Gatev, Thomas, Kepple & Hallett 1999, J Physiol 514:915-928](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.1999.915ad.x). Segment CoMs move in phase with the whole-body CoM, supporting the single inverted-pendulum model of quiet stance. — [Gage et al. 2004, Gait Posture 19:124-132](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0966-6362(03)00037-7)
- **Hof's three mechanisms.** From the multi-segment equations of motion, horizontal CoM acceleration has three inputs: "(1) moving the centre of pressure with respect to the vertical projection of the CoM, (2) counter-rotating segments around the CoM, and (3) applying an external force". Mechanism 2 "can be considerable, e.g. in one-legged standing", where the CoP range is small. — [Hof 2007, J Biomech 40:451-457](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2005.12.016)
- **Robot analogue.** Ankle, hip and stepping push-recovery strategies have been analysed for humanoids with simple models (LIPM, LIPM plus flywheel), with stability boundaries derived for each. Title and DOI were checked; the content is from my knowledge of the paper. — [Stephens 2007, IEEE-RAS Humanoids](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHR.2007.4813931). The flywheel (angular-momentum) channel enlarges the capture region. — [Pratt et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICHR.2006.321385), [US 7,949,430](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7949430)
- **Tails as inertial stabilisers.** Lizards "control the swing of their tails in a measured manner to redirect angular momentum from their bodies to their tails", stabilising sagittal pitch. A lizard-sized robot with an active, feedback-controlled tail reproduced this. A modelled *Velociraptor mongoliensis* showed "a greater tail effectiveness than the Agama lizards". — [Libby et al. 2012, Nature 481:181-184](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10710)
- **Plantar sensation shifts strategy toward the hip.** After plantar anaesthesia, lateral perturbations produced "a relative shift in compensatory torque production from the ankles and trunk to the hips". — [Meyer, Oddsson & De Luca 2004b, Exp Brain Res 157:526-536](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-004-1868-3)
- **Theropod CoM context.** T. rex adult body mass was estimated at about 6000–8000 kg, and the CoM shifted craniodorsally during ontogeny. — [Hutchinson et al. 2011, PLoS ONE 6:e26037](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026037). The craniad CoM position in all T. rex models means it "required some flexion in the limbs to stand still", with how much depending on the CoM location. — [Hutchinson, Ng-Thow-Hing & Anderson 2007, J Theor Biol 246:660-680](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtbi.2007.01.023). Chickens raised with artificial tails (CoM moved posteriorly) stood with a more vertical femur. — [Grossi et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9:e88458](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0088458). Along the bird line, forelimb enlargement influenced CoM position more strongly than tail reduction. — [Allen, Bates, Li & Hutchinson 2013, Nature 497:104-107](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12059)

### Inferences
- **The head–tail axis as a hip-strategy actuator.** In a long-tailed biped, pitching the trunk–tail unit about the hip shifts the whole-body CoM fore–aft relative to the feet, a static "posture" use. Rapid counter-rotation of tail or head generates moments about the CoM, which is Hof's mechanism 2 (dynamic use). Both act without moving the CoP. A policy that corrects forward drift only with ankle or toe torque is using the most range-limited channel. Rewarding or permitting hip/tail counter-rotation, and penalising CoP–edge proximity, would push it toward the cheaper channel that Kuo & Zajac identify.
- **Caveat on transfer.** Human hip-strategy data come from a tall, top-heavy body with the trunk above the hip. A theropod's trunk and tail are roughly horizontal and balanced about the hip, so pitching about the hip moves mass both fore and aft. The CoM shift per radian depends on the head-plus-neck versus tail mass distribution, not on the human 1:3 ankle:hip ratio. Those human ratios should not be copied.
- **Ankle strategy remains the default for quiet stance** in humans (Gatev). For a standing policy the expected pattern is small CoP modulation near the foot centre, with hip/tail recruited for larger or faster disturbances. This is a target behaviour to check in rollouts, for example by decomposing CoM acceleration into Hof's CoP term and counter-rotation term.

### Gaps
- Found no quantitative study of standing (as opposed to leaping) balance strategies in living tailed bipeds (birds, bipedal lizards), and no measurements of tail use for quiet-stance balance.
- No primary data on how the human hip strategy scales when the "upper body" is a horizontal trunk–tail beam.

## 3. Ankle stiffness and passive elements: intrinsic stiffness vs the gravitational toppling stiffness, whether passive stiffness alone can hold an inverted pendulum, damping, and series-elastic or compliant feet and toes in robots

### Takeaway
The critical (toppling) stiffness of an inverted pendulum is K_crit = m·g·h. Human intrinsic ankle stiffness is *below* it: about 91 ± 23% (tiny perturbations; Loram & Lakie 2002), about 64 ± 8% (1° perturbations; Casadio et al. 2005), and falling to about 13–19% for large rotations (Loram et al. 2007). Passive stiffness alone therefore cannot hold the human pendulum. Balance needs active, anticipatory, velocity-sensitive torque modulation, with total effective feedback stiffness about 1.3× mgh plus matching damping (Peterka 2002). In robots, ankle series elasticity low-pass filters ZMP dynamics and has been shown to improve balance recovery when stiffness is matched.

### Cited Findings
- **Loram & Lakie 2002 (direct measurement).** Intrinsic ankle stiffness, measured with small, unobtrusive perturbations that evoked no stretch reflexes, "is 91 ± 23% (mean ± S.D.) of that necessary to provide minimal stabilisation". It was "substantially constant, increasing only slightly with ankle torque", "cannot be neurally regulated in quiet standing", and was attributed "to the foot, Achilles' tendon and aponeurosis rather than the activated calf muscle fibres". The brain "cannot set ankle stiffness and then ignore the control task". — [Loram & Lakie 2002, J Physiol 545:1041-1053](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2002.025049)
- **Casadio et al. 2005.** With 1°, 150 ms step perturbations from a motorised footplate on a force platform, intrinsic ankle stiffness was "64 ± 8% of the critical stiffness". The authors treat 64% (large perturbations) and 91% (tiny perturbations) as the lower and upper bounds for normal sway. — [Casadio, Morasso & Sanguineti 2005, Gait Posture 21:410-424](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gaitpost.2004.05.005)
- **Non-linear passive stiffness (Loram et al. 2007).** Passive calf muscles provide "an extensor torque capable of sustaining unstable balance without tonic activity at a mean CoM-ankle angle of 1.6 deg". Long-range stiffness is 13 ± 2% of mgh and short-range (<0.2°) stiffness 67 ± 8%. Chordal stiffness falls non-linearly "from 101 ± 9% to 19 ± 5% for rotations of 0.03–7 deg". The paper also notes reports elsewhere of substantial intrinsic stiffness (65–90% of mgh), while others put the passive contribution at only 10–15%. — [Loram, Maganaris & Lakie 2007a, J Physiol 584:661-675](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.140046). Series-elastic (tendon/foot) stiffness is about 77 ± 13% of mgh, and short-range passive stiffness originates in the contractile component. — [Loram, Maganaris & Lakie 2007b, J Physiol 584:677-692](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.140053)
- **Stiffness-only control was disputed.** Morasso & Schieppati argued that "physiological values of stiffness of the ankle muscles are insufficient to stabilize the body 'inverted pendulum'". The in-phase CoP–CoM relationship is "determined by physics, not by control patterns". — [Morasso & Schieppati 1999, J Neurophysiol 82:1622-1626](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1999.82.3.1622). A realistic muscle model "demonstrates the mechanical instability of the system when there is no anticipatory control input". — [Morasso & Sanguineti 2002, J Neurophysiol 88:2157-2162](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2002.88.4.2157). **Contradicting view:** Winter et al. estimated stiffness from ankle moment vs sway angle as "safely above the gravitational spring" (R² ≈ 0.92) and proposed simple stiffness control. — [Winter et al. 2001, J Neurophysiol 85:2630-2633](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2001.85.6.2630); [Winter et al. 2003, J Electromyogr Kinesiol 13:49-56](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1050-6411(02)00085-8). The later direct measurements (Loram & Lakie 2002; Casadio 2005; Loram 2007) side with Morasso.
- **Active control is intermittent, ballistic and predictive.** Calf muscles are actively adjusted "2.6 times per second and 2.8 times per unidirectional sway" with 30–300 µm length changes. This is inconsistent with control solely by local reflexes. — [Loram, Maganaris & Lakie 2005, J Physiol 564:295-311](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2004.076307). Balancing a real inverted pendulum used a biphasic "throw and catch" torque pattern. Intrinsic stiffness contributed substantially but "by itself this was insufficient to maintain balance or to control position". — [Loram & Lakie 2002a, J Physiol 540:1111-1124](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2001.013077)
- **Velocity (damping) information matters.** Experimental CoM–EMG cross-correlations matched a PD controller with *high derivative (velocity) gain*, not a low-derivative one. "The actual postural control system … relies notably on velocity information." — [Masani et al. 2003, J Neurophysiol 90:3774-3782](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00730.2002)
- **Feedback stiffness and damping (Peterka 2002).** Model fits gave stiffness "always about 1/3 larger than necessary to resist the destabilizing torque due to gravity" (about 1.33 × mgh). "To maintain postural stability and avoid resonant behavior, an increase in stiffness should be accompanied by a corresponding increase in damping". The higher damping was achieved mainly by reducing the apparent feedback time delay. — [Peterka 2002, J Neurophysiol 88:1097-1118](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2002.88.3.1097). Too much corrective torque relative to sway produced a 1 Hz body oscillation. — [Peterka & Loughlin 2004, J Neurophysiol 91:410-423](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00516.2003)
- **Stiffening under threat.** Standing at the edge of an 81 cm-high surface increased AP stiffness by 27.5% and shifted mean CoP and CoM *backward*, away from the edge. Tibialis anterior activity correlated with stiffness, while soleus/gastrocnemius activity correlated with mean CoP position. — [Carpenter et al. 2001, Exp Brain Res 138:210-218](https://doi.org/10.1007/s002210100681)
- **Feedback gains depend on posture.** In a frontal-plane model with delayed feedback, the ranges of stable gains at different stance widths were "nonoverlapping", so "using a single neural feedback control strategy at both narrow and wide stances could lead to instability". Foot lift-off constraints shrink the stable set further. — [Bingham, Choi & Ting 2011, J Neurophysiol 106:437-448](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00010.2011)
- **Series elasticity in humanoid ankles.** On a humanoid with series elastic ankle actuators, compared against rigid transmissions of matched static stiffness under repeatable impulsive pushes, static responses were the same. However, "series elasticity … low-pass filter[ed] the dynamics of the zero moment point", which simplified the controller's job and gave more stable balance recovery (paraphrased from the abstract page). — [Eljaik, Li, Randazzo, Parmiggiani, Metta, Tsagarakis & Nori 2013, "Quantitative Evaluation of Standing Stabilization Using Stiff and Compliant Actuators", RSS IX](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss09/p08.html)
- **Other robot compliance references** (titles and DOIs checked, abstracts not retrieved): COMAN stabilisation exploiting intrinsic (SEA) and controlled compliance — [Li et al. 2012, ICRA](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICRA.2012.6224705). Series elastic actuators — [Pratt & Williamson 1995, IROS](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.1995.525827). Passivity-based full-body balancing under unknown external forces with contact-force distribution — [Hyon, Hale & Cheng 2007, IEEE T-RO 23:884-898](https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2007.904896).
- **Passive weight support in a bird.** Flamingo cadavers "could passively support body weight on one leg without any muscle activity" in a stable joint posture, but "could not be stably held in a two-legged pose". Live flamingos on one leg placed "the point of force application directly under the distal joint, reducing the need for muscular joint torque". — [Chang & Ting 2017, Biol Lett 13:20160948](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948). Ostrich intertarsal ("ankle") joint: a passive ligamentous engage-disengage mechanism provides bi-directional resistance and joint stabilisation. — [Schaller et al. 2009, J Anat 214:830-847](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7580.2009.01083.x)

### Inferences
- **Design number for the simulator: normalise ankle stiffness to mgh.** For the T. rex model, compute K_crit = m·g·h, where h is the height of the whole-body CoM above the effective ankle/MTP pivot, in N·m/rad (summed over both legs in double stance). Then express any passive ankle or toe spring as a percentage of K_crit. A passive spring at about 60–90% of K_crit (the human range) does not stabilise on its own but greatly slows the toppling mode: the unstable eigenvalue scales with √((mgh − K)/I). A spring above 100% would make the pendulum passively stable but with low damping. Peterka's human fit suggests about 130% total effective stiffness *with matching damping*.
- **Damping matters as much as stiffness.** Human data (Masani: high velocity gain; Peterka: damping must rise with stiffness) suggest that a policy controlling position-servo actuators needs joint-level damping, or velocity-based CoM feedback, in the observation. Otherwise high stiffness produces oscillation and noise amplification. In MuJoCo, joint `damping` and `stiffness` attributes (with `springref`) are the cheap way to give a passive baseline. For a position actuator, kp acts as stiffness about the commanded target and kv/damping as damping. This is a modelling inference, not from a cited source.
- **A passive "set-point" spring that centres the CoP.** Loram 2007's finding that passive calf muscles hold the body without tonic activity at a CoM-ankle angle of 1.6° suggests an alternative design: give the ankle/MTP a passive spring whose rest angle puts the CoM over the middle of the foot. Then "doing nothing" drifts the CoP back toward the centre rather than letting it run to the toe edge.
- **Joint-space action noise.** Series elasticity low-pass filters ZMP dynamics (Eljaik 2013). Physical compliance plus damping between the actuator and the foot would likewise filter joint-space action noise before it reaches the CoP. Rigid position servos pass noise straight through to the CoP.
- **Gain retuning with posture** (Bingham 2011): a policy trained at one lean or posture may not be robust at another. If the policy drifts forward mid-training, its implicit gains were tuned for a different operating point. This argues for keeping the operating point fixed (CoP-centring reward) rather than letting it wander.
- **Transfer caveat.** Human stiffness values are for a plantigrade ankle spanning a 50–60 cm-long lever (shank and foot). In a digitigrade theropod the balancing "ankle" is a chain of intertarsal, MTP and digit joints with a long, elevated metatarsus. Percent-of-mgh normalisation still transfers, but which joint carries it does not: the MTP/toe joints act on the CoP directly, while the intertarsal joint acts through the metatarsus.

### Gaps
- No primary robotics study was found that gives a passive ankle or toe stiffness as a percentage of mgh for a standing humanoid. Eljaik 2013 matched static stiffness between conditions, but the numeric value was not retrieved.
- Li et al. 2012 (COMAN) and Hyon et al. 2007 abstracts were not retrieved; only titles and DOIs were verified.
- No data on intrinsic ankle or MTP stiffness in large birds during standing (the flamingo and ostrich studies are qualitative or one-legged).

## 4. Sensing: plantar sensation in human balance, and foot force/torque or pressure sensing on biped robots for CoP feedback

### Takeaway
The foot sole acts as a "dynamometric map" of where the load sits. Stimulating forefoot receptors makes people lean *backward*, and stimulating the heel makes them lean forward, so plantar input actively centres the CoP. Removing plantar sensation alone has modest effects on quiet sway (COP velocity +11–12%, eyes closed). It degrades the *perception* of standing position near quiet stance, biases reproduced positions *forward*, and shifts responses from ankle to hip. Combined sensory loss (neuropathy) raises instability by 66–117%. On robots, CoP/ZMP is measured with ankle or sole force/torque sensors and fed back through foot force (ankle torque/damping) controllers. A policy lacking foot-load or CoP observations has to infer CoP from proprioception and inertial cues.

### Cited Findings
- **Dynamometric map (Kavounoudias et al. 1998).** High-frequency, low-amplitude vibration of anterior and/or posterior sole areas produced whole-body tilts. Their direction depended on the area stimulated and "was always opposite to the vibration-simulated pressure increase". Cutaneous messages combined by "vector addition". — [Kavounoudias, Roll & Roll 1998, NeuroReport 9:3247-3252](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001756-199810050-00021)
- **Foot sole plus muscle proprioception (Kavounoudias et al. 2001).** Forefoot vibration and tibialis anterior tendon vibration produced backward and forward body tilts respectively, "the amplitude of which was proportional to the vibration frequency" (20–80 Hz). Tactile input dominated at low frequencies and proprioceptive input at high. Combined stimuli summed as vectors. — [Kavounoudias, Roll & Roll 2001, J Physiol 532:869-878](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7793.2001.0869e.x)
- **Plantar anaesthesia in unperturbed stance (Meyer et al. 2004a).** The effects of foot-sole anaesthesia were "generally small", mostly increases in COP velocity. During bipedal stance, significant effects appeared only with eyes closed: COP velocity +11–12% and shear-force RMS +13%. Unipedal stance also showed AP COP median frequency +36%. "Magnitude of COP displacement was unaffected." Forefoot anaesthesia mainly affected mediolateral control, whole-sole anaesthesia AP control. The authors conclude plantar sensation sets "background muscle activity" and is "of moderate importance" for quiet stance. — [Meyer, Oddsson & De Luca 2004a, Exp Brain Res 156:505-512](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-003-1804-y)
- **Plantar anaesthesia in perturbed stance (Meyer et al. 2004b).** Torque shifted from ankles and trunk to hips. "Plantar cutaneous afferents play an important role in the shaping of dynamic postural responses." — [Meyer, Oddsson & De Luca 2004b](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-004-1868-3)
- **Stepping responses.** Hypothermic plantar anaesthesia revealed roles in "sensing posterior stability limits during initiation of backward steps" and in controlling heel contact and weight transfer at the end of forward steps. — [Perry, McIlroy & Maki 2000, Brain Res 877:401-406](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0006-8993(00)02712-8). In a forward-fall recovery step, motor activity "was more affected by loss of muscle proprioceptive afferents than by loss of plantar cutaneous afferents". — [Thoumie & Do 1996, Exp Brain Res 110:289-297](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00228559)
- **Cooling the sole biases perceived position forward.** Cooling the sole to 1 °C for 30–40 min reduced plantar sensitivity. Mean quiet-stance CoP was 45.7% of foot length from the heel. Reproduction errors were largest at the 40/50/60 %FL references (near quiet stance) and larger still when cooled. The "50% and 60% reference positions were reproduced significantly further forward in the cooled than in the normal condition." — [Fujiwara et al. 2003, Percept Mot Skills 96:381-399](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.2003.96.2.381). Cooling the heel moved the most-backward-leaning position further back and increased error at 25 %FL, suggesting heel pressure is the key cue for the posterior limit. — [Asai & Inaoka 2021, Neurosci Lett 750:135752](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neulet.2021.135752)
- **Proprioceptive acuity.** Thresholds for perceiving sway were "typically 0.003 rad at a velocity of 0.001 rad s⁻¹". Leg proprioception alone matched full-sensory thresholds, while vestibular thresholds were "an order of magnitude greater". — [Fitzpatrick & McCloskey 1994, J Physiol 478:173-186](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1994.sp020240)
- **Sensory reweighting.** Stance relies on visual, vestibular and proprioceptive feedback with reweighting: increasing reliance on vestibular cues as surface-tilt amplitude increased. — [Peterka 2002](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.2002.88.3.1097). Pressure/CoP feedback added to a visual target did not improve stance over vision alone. — [Duarte & Zatsiorsky 2002, Exp Brain Res 146:60-69](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-002-1154-1)
- **Combined somatosensory loss.** Patients with diabetic sensory neuropathy showed "between 66 and 117% more instability" than controls, worst with vision or vestibular cues absent or degraded. — [Simoneau et al. 1994, Diabetes Care 17:1411-1421](https://doi.org/10.2337/diacare.17.12.1411)
- **Enhancing plantar input.** Sub-sensory noise from vibrating insoles reduced 7 of 8 sway parameters in young and all sway variables in elderly participants. — [Priplata et al. 2003, Lancet 362:1123-1124](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(03)14470-4)
- **Birds.** In ostriches, slight variations in 4th-toe position affect CoP origin, and "modulation of 4th toe pressure on the substrate allows correction of balance". Walking and running CoP trajectories were J-shaped. — [Schaller et al. 2011, J Exp Biol 214:1123-1130](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.043596)
- **Robots: measured CoP/ZMP and force feedback.**
  - HRP-4C walking stabilisation adds "a body posture controller and foot force controllers on the joint position servo". The robot can then be treated as a linear inverted pendulum with "ZMP delay", enabling a tracking controller (abstract, via AIST bibliography). — [Kajita et al. 2010, IROS](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2010.5651082); [AIST bib](https://unit.aist.go.jp/jrl-22022/assets/bib/kajita_iros_2010.bib)
  - HRP-2 carries force/torque sensors at the ankles, from which the ZMP/CoP is computed. This is from my knowledge of the HRP-2 design paper; the abstract was not retrieved, and a patent in the search results describes a six-axis force sensor on each ankle used to compute ZMP. — [Kaneko et al. 2004, ICRA](https://doi.org/10.1109/ROBOT.2004.1307969)
  - Contact-force optimisation for posture and balance on torque-controlled bipeds (title checked). — [Ott, Roa & Hirzinger 2011, Humanoids](https://doi.org/10.1109/Humanoids.2011.6100882)
  - CoP–ZMP equivalence and the forces acting on a biped, including measurement. — [Sardain & Bessonnet 2004](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMCA.2004.832811)

### Inferences
- **Forward drift without foot sensing has a human parallel.** With plantar sensation reduced, humans reproduce near-quiet-stance positions *further forward* (Fujiwara 2003). Without heel input they lose the posterior limit cue (Asai 2021; Perry 2000). A policy with no foot-load or CoP observation is in a similar position. Near the foot centre, joint-angle and IMU signals change very little as the CoP moves (cf. low perceptibility at 40–60 %FL), so nothing in the observation pushes back against slow forward drift until the toe edge is reached. Adding per-foot vertical force and CoP (or heel/toe contact forces) to the observation is the robot analogue of the plantar "dynamometric map".
- **What to measure.** Per-foot CoP as a fraction of foot length (heel = 0, toe tip = 1), whole-body XcoM margin b to each edge, and fore/aft load split (heel vs toe or pad vs digits). These map directly onto the human literature (Fujiwara's %FL; Hof's b). Logging both the CoP and the CoM projection separates "leaning" (CoM forward) from "pushing" (CoP forward with CoM centred).
- **Plantar sensing helps but is not sufficient.** Humans with anaesthetised soles still stand with only small sway changes, because proprioception and vision compensate (Meyer 2004a; Thoumie & Do 1996). For the robot, foot sensing is most useful as a reward-shaping or observation cue for *where* the CoP sits. Velocity feedback (Masani) and stiffness/damping (section 3) handle the stabilisation itself.
- **Transfer caveat.** Theropod and bird feet put load on digit pads and the MTP pad rather than heel plus forefoot. The analogous "map" would be MTP pad vs digit tips, and the ostrich data suggest toe-specific pressure modulation is used for balance correction.

### Gaps
- Meyer 2004a gives percentage changes in COP velocity but not absolute values. No source here quantifies how much plantar anaesthesia shifts *mean* CoP position in quiet stance, only perceived and reproduced positions (Fujiwara).
- No sources were found here on reinforcement-learning biped policies with vs without foot-force/CoP observations (not searched; outside the physiology/robot-control scope).
- The HRP-2 sensor placement statement relies on background knowledge plus a patent snippet, not the retrieved abstract.

## 5. Typical standing posture: where healthy humans keep the CoP in quiet stance, the forward lean of quiet stance, and the cost of standing near the toe edge

### Takeaway
Healthy humans stand with the mean CoP at about 46% of foot length from the heel (individual range about 31–58 %FL). That sits roughly mid-way within a voluntary range of about 20–80 %FL, with the CoM slightly ahead of the ankle, so tonic calf activity is needed. Leaning away from that point raises sway area, muscle activity and toe-flexor recruitment, and reduces the margin to the edge. Under threat at an edge, humans move the CoP *away* from the edge and stiffen. Quiet stance is therefore a deliberately centred, slightly forward-of-ankle posture, not an edge-seeking one.

### Cited Findings
- **Mean quiet-stance CoP: 45.7% of foot length from the heel** (16 undergraduates). — [Fujiwara et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.2003.96.2.381)
- **Individual quiet-stance CoP ranges 31–58 %FL.** Humans can hold reference positions from 20 to 80 %FL. The regression intersection for the least perceptible position was 53 %FL. — [Fujiwara et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.2114/jpa2.29.197)
- **Forward lean / CoM ahead of the ankle.** "Tonic ankle extensor torque is required to support the centre of mass (CoM) forward of the ankles". Passive calf muscles alone could sustain the posture "at a mean CoM-ankle angle of 1.6 deg". — [Loram, Maganaris & Lakie 2007a](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.140046). During stance the COG was anterior to the ankle and knee and posterior to the hip; standing with feet together increased anterior COG position. — [Danis et al. 1998, Phys Ther 78:502-517](https://doi.org/10.1093/ptj/78.5.502). Soleus "was tonically active during normal stance". — [Schieppati et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0)
- **Cost of leaning.** Sway area "was minimal during normal stance with EO and increased progressively when the subjects leant forward or backward". Forward leaning increased soleus EMG and recruited FDB (toe flexor). — [Schieppati et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0168-5597(94)90031-0). "The variability of the COP displacement … increased when subjects occupied leaning postures." Without vision, drifts were "larger for COP targets farther away from the neutral position". — [Duarte & Zatsiorsky 2002, Exp Brain Res 146:60-69](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-002-1154-1)
- **Functional limits.** Voluntary AP CoP range is about 60% of foot length in young adults (Schieppati 1994; King 1994 FBOS 0.60 ± 0.07). In King et al., forward lean made up 66% and backward 34% of the FBOS. — [King et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1093/geronj/49.6.m258)
- **Response to threat: move away from the edge and stiffen.** Standing at the edge of a raised platform raised AP stiffness 27.5% and shifted mean CoP/CoM backward (away from the edge). It also increased COP mean power frequency and decreased COP/COM displacement amplitude. — [Carpenter et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/s002210100681)
- **Birds stand with the load under the distal joint.** One-legged flamingos placed "the point of force application directly under the distal joint", minimising muscular joint torque, and showed markedly reduced sway when quiescent. — [Chang & Ting 2017](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2016.0948)

### Inferences
- **Target operating point for the T. rex.** By analogy, keep per-foot mean CoP near the middle of the *usable* contact length, roughly 40–55% of the contact-patch length from its rear edge. Treat the outer about 20% at each end as a reserve. Keeping the voluntary range to about 60% of the foot (Schieppati; King) and standing at about 46 %FL (Fujiwara) leaves forward and backward margins of roughly a quarter to a third of foot length each. For the digitigrade foot, "foot length" should be the contact patch (MTP pad to digit tips), not the anatomical foot including the elevated metatarsus. This is an analogy, not a measured theropod value.
- **"Cost of the toe edge" in RL terms.** Near the edge (i) the margin b and the absorbable velocity disturbance b·ω0 go to zero (section 1). (ii) Corrective CoP authority exists in only one direction (backward). (iii) Effort rises: in humans, soleus plus toe flexors (Schieppati). (iv) Sway variability rises (Duarte & Zatsiorsky). A reward term on CoP-to-centre distance (or on XcoM margin) and a cost on toe-flexor/ankle effort would mirror the human costs that keep quiet stance centred.
- **The forward lean in humans is small and passive-friendly.** About 1.6° of CoM-ankle angle can be held passively (Loram 2007a). Human quiet stance has a slight forward lean, so the CoP sits just ahead of the ankle while still near the foot's middle (about 46 %FL). The healthy forward lean is therefore a few degrees, with the CoP mid-foot, not a lean that carries the CoP to the toes.
- **Transfer caveat.** In a long-tailed digitigrade biped, the CoM sits close to the hip, and the hip lies over or near the contact patch (Hutchinson 2007: T. rex needed some limb flexion to stand, depending on CoM position). The human "CoM slightly ahead of the ankle" rule maps to "CoM projection over the mid-contact patch, slightly forward of the MTP pad". Tail/head posture is the main knob for placing it there (Grossi 2014; Allen 2013).

### Gaps
- No direct measurement of quiet-stance CoP location (as a fraction of contact length) was found for large birds or other digitigrade bipeds standing on two legs.
- No explicit metabolic or EMG cost-vs-CoP-position curve was retrieved. Schieppati 1994 and Duarte & Zatsiorsky 2002 give qualitative or relative increases in EMG and sway area with lean, without a tabulated function in the abstracts.
- The "Smith 1957" classic on ankle forces in standing (J Anat 91:545, [PMC1244908](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1244908/)) has no abstract on PubMed. Its quantitative gravity-line position relative to the ankle was not extracted. Winter's review [Winter 1995, Gait Posture 3:193-214](https://doi.org/10.1016/0966-6362(96)82849-9) was identified, but its contents were not retrieved for specific numbers.
